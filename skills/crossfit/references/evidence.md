# What the evidence supports

Use this file to say how strong the support is for a claim. Full citations are in
[sources.md](sources.md).

## Strength labels

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Moderate** | Systematic reviews or meta-analyses with consistent findings. Usually not in CrossFit athletes. |
| **Limited** | Small studies, surveys, cross-sectional data, or a few cohorts. |
| **Very limited** | One small study, a case series, or a finding borrowed from another sport. |
| **None** | No study found. Official guidance or coaching practice only. |
| **Not supported** | Studies or analyses looked, and found no support for the claim. |

No claim in this skill rests on strong evidence from trials in CrossFit athletes.
Say so when an athlete asks how sure you are.

Official guidance is CrossFit's own position. It tells you what CrossFit teaches.
It is not evidence that the method works better than another.

## By topic

### Injury

| Claim | Strength | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Injury rates in CrossFit are in the range of other training and sports | Limited | A focused review of 3 retrospective cohorts found rates "comparable or lower than" weightlifting, distance running, rugby, and gymnastics; the authors call the certainty "questionable" (Klimek et al., 2018). A systematic review of 25 studies (12,079 people) found incidence from 0.2 to 18.9 per 1,000 hours and a mean prevalence of 35.3% (Rodríguez et al., 2022). |
| The shoulder, back, and knee are the most common sites | Limited | Surveys: 3,049 people (Feito et al., 2018); 386 people (Weisenthal et al., 2014); review of 25 studies (Rodríguez et al., 2022). All self-reported. |
| Coach supervision goes with fewer injuries | Limited | Lower injury rate with trainer involvement in 386 people (Weisenthal et al., 2014); listed as a risk factor in Rodríguez et al. (2022). Associations, not proven cause. |
| Injury is more often reported in the first year | Limited | Survey of 3,049 people (Feito et al., 2018). Training hours were assumed. |
| Competitors report more injuries | Limited | 40% against 19% in 191 athletes (Montalvo et al., 2017). Competitors also trained more hours. |
| Switching between prescribed and scaled loads goes with more injury | Limited | One low-bias cohort, odds ratio 3.5, in a review of 3 prospective cohorts (Mehrab et al., 2023). |
| Doing other sports does not change injury risk | Limited on this scale | The review graded it "strong" in its own best-evidence synthesis, but it rests on 3 cohorts (Mehrab et al., 2023). |

### Rhabdomyolysis

| Claim | Strength | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Beginners made up 6 of 11 rhabdomyolysis cases at one hospital | Very limited | Of 523 patients with CrossFit-related injuries at one hospital from 2010 to 2016, 11 had rhabdomyolysis, and 6 of those 11 were beginners (Hopkins et al., 2019). The true rate is unknown. A published comment disputes the conclusions (Dominski et al., 2019). |
| Warning signs and the need for urgent care | None (official guidance) | L1 p. 153. Follow it anyway: the risk is serious. |

### Monitoring

| Claim | Strength | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Session effort times minutes tracks training load | Moderate in other sports; limited in this training | Review of 36 studies, mostly team and endurance sports (Haddad et al., 2017). In this training: group agreement r = 0.889 with heart-rate load, but only 52% agreement for single ratings, in 25 adults (Crawford et al., 2018); 8 men (Falk Neto et al., 2020). |
| A load ratio predicts injury | Not supported | The acute-to-chronic workload ratio "adds noise and creates statistical artifacts"; no evidence supports it for reducing injury (Impellizzeri et al., 2020). An analysis paper, not new data. |
| Weekly heart-rate variability means are more useful than single days | Limited | 10 runners (Plews et al., 2013); at least 3 valid days a week (Plews et al., 2014). Morning chest-strap recordings. |
| A change within about half a standard deviation is ordinary | Limited | 40 runners (Vesterinen et al., 2016). Morning chest-strap recordings. |
| Heart-rate variability after a CrossFit workout recovers within 2 hours | Very limited | 10 men, short and 15-minute workouts (Kliszczewicz et al., 2018). Says nothing about several days. |

### Recovery

| Claim | Strength | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Markers return to baseline within 48 hours of a single workout | Very limited | 12 trained people (Timón et al., 2019). |
| Two days in a row did not reduce squat power | Very limited | 9 trained men (Tibana et al., 2016). |
| Sets to failure slow recovery for 24 to 48 hours | Very limited | 10 trained men, bench press and squat (Morán-Navarro et al., 2017). Not CrossFit. |

### Strength and conditioning together

| Claim | Strength | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Aerobic training added to lifting does not meaningfully reduce maximal strength or muscle size | Moderate | 43 studies (Schumann et al., 2022). Not mixed-modal workouts. |
| Explosive strength is reduced, more in the same session | Moderate | Schumann et al. (2022); Wilson et al. (2012), 21 studies. |
| Lifting before endurance work in one session gives slightly more leg strength | Limited | About 4 kg on average (Murlasits et al., 2018). |
| How much interference happens inside a CrossFit week | None | No study measures it. |

### Strength progression

| Claim | Strength | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Untrained people gain from moderate loads; trained people need heavier loads and more volume for smaller gains | Moderate | Rhea et al. (2003), 140 studies; Peterson et al. (2004, 2005). Pooled averages. |
| Heavier loads, above 80%, give the most strength | Moderate | 178 trials (Currier et al., 2023). Mostly short trials. |
| One hard set 2 to 3 times a week still raises strength in trained men | Limited | 5 studies (Androulakis-Korakakis et al., 2020). |
| Add 2 to 10% load when the athlete beats the target reps | Consensus | ACSM (2009). Partly expert opinion. |
| Weekly kilogram gains for CrossFit athletes | None | No study. |

### Gymnastics skills

| Claim | Strength | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Strict pull-up before kipping; strict muscle-up before kipping | None (official guidance) | L1 pp. 219, 232. |
| Banded pull-ups, negatives, kipping drills, and muscle-up or handstand push-up progressions work | None | No trials in CrossFit athletes. |
| Twice-weekly pull-up training of several kinds raises pull-up strength | Very limited | 30 climbers, 5 weeks, 2 to 5% gains (Vigouroux and Devise, 2024). |
| Kipping shifts work from the biceps to the trunk and hips | Very limited | 11 athletes, one session (Dinunzio et al., 2019). Not a training or injury study. |
| Wide and reverse grips show shoulder movement linked with impingement risk | Very limited | 11 people, strict pull-ups only, no injury outcomes (Prinold and Bull, 2016). |
| Double-under technique changes with fatigue | Very limited | 10 athletes (Bruce et al., 2017). |

### Programming and scaling

| Claim | Strength | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| "Preserve the stimulus" scaling gives the same adaptation as the prescribed workout | None | Official guidance (L1 p. 77). Untested. |
| Three days on, one day off beats other schedules | None | CrossFit's own observation (L1 pp. 71–72). CrossFit.com never used the template. |
| A two-week taper with 41 to 60% less volume helps performance | Moderate in other sports; none in CrossFit | 27 studies, before CrossFit research existed (Bosquet et al., 2007). |
| No good evidence that CrossFit improves fitness more than other training | Not supported | A review of 31 articles found "only two studies had a high level of evidence at low risk of bias", and its meta-analysis "found no significant effects" (Claudino et al., 2018). Recreational CrossFit participants did not differ from active adults doing traditional lifting and cardio, in a cross-sectional study of 23 people (Mangine et al., 2020). |

### Benchmarks

| Claim | Strength | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Typical benchmark scores | Limited | 10,000 self-reported, unverified public profiles from 2017; scaled entries not separated (Mangine et al., 2018). |
| Strength predicts Fran and Grace | Very limited | 14 athletes (Butcher et al., 2015). Nothing predicted Cindy. |

## What the evidence does not establish

State these plainly when they matter.

- That CrossFit is safer or riskier than other training. Most data are
  self-reported surveys with assumed hours.
- Which risk factors cause injury. Only 3 prospective cohorts exist.
- The true rate of rhabdomyolysis in CrossFit.
- That any training-load number predicts injury in CrossFit.
- That one athlete's session rating matches their heart-rate effort on a given day.
- Which rating scale and timing are best for CrossFit. Foster's whole-session 0–10
  method and Crawford's 6–20 workout-only method give numbers on different scales.
  Never mix them for one athlete.
- That scaled workouts give the same adaptation as prescribed ones.
- That one weekly schedule is better than another.
- That benchmark norms describe verified, standard-quality reps.
- How much strength and conditioning interfere inside mixed workouts.
- Exact strength gains to expect per week.
- How best to progress gymnastics skills.
- How many days of recovery a given workout needs, for novices, women, masters
  athletes, or high-volume weeks.
- How to taper for the Open.
- What predicts Open ranking.

## How to say it

Bad: "Studies show kipping is bad for your shoulders."

Good: "No study has tested whether kipping pull-ups cause shoulder injuries. Two
small studies looked at how pull-ups move the body. One, of 11 athletes, found that
kipping shifts work from the arms to the trunk and hips. The other, of 11 people,
found that wide and reverse grips on strict pull-ups moved the shoulder blade in
ways linked with impingement risk. Neither measured injuries. CrossFit advises a
strict pull-up before kipping (official). I suggest a shoulder-width grip while you
learn. That is coaching practice."
