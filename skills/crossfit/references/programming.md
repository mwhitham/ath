# Programming

This file explains how to build a week and a block of weeks. A plan is saved as one
`training_plan` and one `planned_session` per session. Each session states its
purpose. A complete example is in [example-block.md](example-block.md).

## What CrossFit says programming is for

- **Official.** CrossFit's prescription is "constantly varied, high-intensity
  functional movement" (L1 p. 2). Intensity is defined as power: work divided by
  time (L1 pp. 2, 36).
- **Official.** The aim is more work capacity "across broad time and modal domains"
  (L1 p. 3). In plain words: the athlete gets better at many kinds of work, from a
  few seconds to long efforts.
- **Official.** "Routine is the enemy of progress and broad adaptation" (L1 p. 30).
  The larger gain comes from the event or skill the athlete would least like to see
  (L1 p. 33).
- **Official.** Programming "is just one piece of an effective program". Technique,
  coaching, environment, nutrition, and the ability to work hard likely matter more
  (O'Connor, 2023).

## The three modalities

**Official** (L1 p. 73):

| Code | Modality | What it develops | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| M | Monostructural ("cardio") | Heart and lung capacity, stamina | Run, row, bike, jump rope |
| G | Gymnastics (body weight) | Body control, coordination, balance, upper-body and trunk strength | Air squat, pull-up, push-up, dip, handstand push-up, rope climb, muscle-up, sit-up, lunge, jump |
| W | Weightlifting (any external load) | Strength, power, hip and leg capacity | Deadlift, clean, press, snatch, clean and jerk, medicine ball, kettlebell swing |

"A strength and conditioning regimen devoid of gymnastics practice and skills is
deficient" (L1 p. 74).

## Single, couplet, and triplet workouts

**Official** (L1 pp. 74–76, Table 3):

| Elements | Priority | Typical structure | Intensity | Work and rest |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| One (single) | The element | M: one long effort. G: practice one skill. W: one heavy lift. | M: long, slow. G: high skill. W: heavy. | Recovery does not limit the work. |
| Two (couplet) | The task: work is fixed, time varies | Two elements repeated 3 to 5 times for time | Two moderately to very hard elements | Managing work and rest is critical. "The first round is hard but possible." |
| Three (triplet) | Time: time is fixed, work varies | Three elements repeated for about 20 minutes, scored in rounds | Three light to moderate elements | Managing work and rest matters less. |

The single G day is for a skill that "might not be yet suitable for inclusion in a
timed workout" (L1 p. 74). This is where an athlete builds a gymnastics skill
before it appears in conditioning.

## Time domains

**Official** sample time domains (O'Connor, 2023):

| Name | Length |
|---|---|
| Short | Under 5 minutes |
| Couplet | 7 to 10 minutes |
| Triplet | 8 to 15, or 10 to 15 minutes |
| Long | 20 minutes or more |

**Official.** A generalist week aims for "one heavy-focused session, one short
workout, one long workout, and three classic workouts". Classic workouts are under
15 minutes: couplets, triplets, and high-power pairings (O'Connor, 2023).

**Official.** "Two-part workouts are not prioritized due to affecting intensity
negatively on one or both sections" (O'Connor, 2023).

**Official.** The energy systems (L1 p. 34): the phosphagen system powers about
the first 10 seconds; the glycolytic system peaks at about 60 seconds and ends near
120 seconds; the oxidative (aerobic) system supports longer efforts at lower power.

## The theoretical template

The Level 1 Training Guide reprints "A Theoretical Template for CrossFit's
Programming" (L1 pp. 71–76). The guide notes that it was "Originally published in
February 2003" (L1 p. 71).

- **Official.** "CrossFit.com has never used this template for its programming."
  Its purpose "is as much descriptive as prescriptive" (L1 p. 71).
- **Official.** It shows a three-days-on, one-day-off pattern over 12 days, and a
  five-days-on, two-days-off pattern for people bound to a work week. "If life is
  easier with the five-on, two-off pattern, do not hesitate to employ it"
  (L1 p. 72).
- **Official.** Another pattern the guide likes: warm-up; 3 to 5 sets of 3 to 5
  reps of a fundamental lift; a 10-minute gymnastics circuit; then 2 to 10 minutes
  of hard conditioning. "There is nothing sacred in this pattern" (L1 pp. 30–31).
- **Research.** No study compares these schedules with others.

Use the template to check a plan for variety: are M, G, and W all present, and do
single, couplet, and triplet days all appear? Do not present it as CrossFit's fixed
routine. Do not copy its day order into a plan without a reason.

## Strength, skill, and conditioning in one plan

### Strength

- **Research.** In meta-analyses, untrained people gained most strength near 60% of
  their one-repetition maximum (the most they can lift once), about 3 days a week
  per muscle group. Trained people gained most near 80%, about 2 days a week
  (Rhea et al., 2003; 140 studies). Athletes gained most near 85%, 2 days a week
  (Peterson et al., 2004). These are pooled averages, not prescriptions.
- **Research.** Higher loads, above 80% of the one-repetition maximum, gave the most
  strength in a network meta-analysis of 178 trials (Currier et al., 2023). The
  abstract does not split novices from trained lifters.
- **Research.** One hard set of 6 to 12 reps, 2 to 3 times a week, raised squat and
  bench press in trained men over 8 to 12 weeks (Androulakis-Korakakis et al., 2020;
  5 studies). The authors call the gain "suboptimal, yet significant".
- **Research.** The American College of Sports Medicine position stand advises
  adding 2 to 10% load when the athlete can do one or two reps more than the
  target (ACSM, 2009). It is a consensus document, partly expert opinion, and over
  15 years old.
- **Practice.** In a CrossFit week, one or two heavy exposures per main lift is
  enough for most developing athletes. Stop sets one to three reps short of failure.

### Strength and conditioning together

- **Research.** Adding aerobic training to lifting did not meaningfully reduce gains
  in maximal strength or muscle size in a meta-analysis of 43 studies. Explosive
  strength was reduced, more when both were in the same session than when they
  were at least 3 hours apart (Schumann et al., 2022). The studies did not use mixed
  CrossFit workouts.
- **Research.** Lifting before endurance work in the same session gave slightly more
  lower-body strength gain, about 4 kg (Murlasits et al., 2018).
- **Practice.** When strength is a priority, put the heavy lift first in the
  session, before conditioning.

### Gymnastics skill

- **Official.** Develop a strict pull-up before kipping pull-ups (L1 p. 219), and a
  strict muscle-up before kipping muscle-ups (L1 p. 232).
- **Research.** No trials test banded pull-ups, negatives, kipping drills, or
  muscle-up and handstand push-up progressions in CrossFit athletes. Climbers who
  did slow-lowering, holding, or explosive pull-up training twice a week for 5
  weeks all gained 2 to 5% in pull-up strength (Vigouroux and Devise, 2024; 30
  climbers).
- **Practice.** Practise a skill often, in short sets, while fresh, and stop before
  form fails. Keep sets one or two reps short of failure on most days.
- **Practice.** Progress one variable at a time: reps per set, then sets, then a
  harder version.

### Recovery between sessions

- **Research.** After single CrossFit workouts, blood markers and a simple
  performance test were back to baseline within 48 hours in 12 trained people
  (Timón et al., 2019). Sets taken to failure slowed recovery for 24 to 48 hours in
  10 trained men, compared with the same volume not taken to failure
  (Morán-Navarro et al., 2017).
- **Official.** Hitting the same body region on back-to-back days raises overuse
  risk (O'Connor, 2025).
- **Practice.** Do not load the same pattern hard on two days in a row. Keep at
  least one full rest day a week.

## Procedure: build a week

1. **Fix the fixed points.** Mark class days, work days, rest days, and the time
   available each day. Classes come first if the athlete pays for them. See
   [class-programming.md](class-programming.md).
2. **Choose one or two priorities** from the assessment. **Official.** "Our goal is
   not to solve for every deficiency in the next two workouts" (O'Connor, 2023).
3. **Fill the week to this checklist.** Each line is **Practice** unless marked.
   - At least one heavy strength exposure (Official: one heavy-focused session,
     O'Connor, 2023).
   - At least two short practice exposures for each priority skill.
   - Conditioning across short, classic, and long time domains.
   - Some monostructural work each week.
   - M, G, and W all present.
   - No pattern loaded hard on two consecutive days.
   - At least one full rest day.
4. **Write each session.** For every session give:
   - its **purpose** in one sentence;
   - its **intended stimulus**: time domain, how hard, and what should limit the
     athlete (breathing, or a muscle group);
   - its **blocks** with sets, reps, loads, distances, and times;
   - its **scaling** rule, and its **route back** (see [scaling.md](scaling.md));
   - what to **record** afterwards.
5. **Check the dose.** Compare planned minutes and sessions with the last 3 to 4
   weeks in the record. **Research.** No load number has been shown to predict
   injury. The acute-to-chronic workload ratio, which compares this week's load with
   recent weeks, is not supported for that use (Impellizzeri et al., 2020).
   **Practice.** Add no more than one new session with hard conditioning or heavy
   lifting at a time. A short skill session kept far from failure adds less
   fatigue and can be added alongside it. Keep new sessions short at first.
6. **State assumptions.** List anything you guessed, such as class content.

## Procedure: build a block

A block is a run of weeks with one main aim.

1. **Length.** **Official.** Targeted blocks last "six to eight weeks", bookended by
   testing (O'Connor, 2023). A shorter block is allowed. Say that a short block can
   only show the direction of change.
2. **Test at the start.** Use a recent record if it matches the retest conditions.
   Otherwise test in week 1.
3. **Progress in the middle weeks.** Change one variable at a time.
   - Strength: add load when target reps are exceeded (ACSM, 2009), or add a set.
   - Gymnastics: more reps per set, then more sets, then a harder version.
   - Conditioning: faster pace at the same volume, or less rest, in the same time
     domain.
4. **Make the last week lighter before a retest.** **Practice**, borrowed from taper
   research in other sports (Bosquet et al., 2007): cut volume by 41 to 60% and
   keep intensity. No CrossFit study tests this. If you cut less, label it a
   hypothesis and give the reason.
5. **Retest under the same conditions.** Same version, loads, standards, time of
   day, and position in the session. See [benchmarks.md](benchmarks.md).
6. **Plan the review points.** One weekly review per week (see
   [weekly-review.md](weekly-review.md)).

## Session patterns by purpose

These are **Practice** examples. Adjust them to the athlete.

| Purpose | Pattern | What should limit the athlete |
|---|---|---|
| Heavy strength | Warm-up, 3 to 5 sets of 3 to 5 of one lift, long rests | The muscles, not breathing |
| Gymnastics skill | Warm-up, 15 to 25 minutes of one skill in short sets, every minute on the minute (start a set at the top of each minute) or with full rest | Form, never fatigue |
| Short and intense | Under 5 minutes, one or two movements, light enough to move fast | Breathing and power |
| Classic couplet or triplet | 7 to 15 minutes, loads that allow the first round unbroken | Breathing; sets broken only a little |
| Long | 20 minutes or more at a pace the athlete can hold | Pacing and breathing |
| Monostructural intervals | Run or row repeats with set rest | Pace held across repeats |

## Writing a planned session record

- `title`: what the session is, such as "Gymnastics: strict pulling and hanging core".
- `activity_type`: `crossfit`.
- `scheduled_date` in `YYYY-MM-DD`, or `scheduled_start_at` in UTC.
- One block per part. Use clear `block_type` words: `warm_up`, `strength`,
  `skill`, `conditioning`, `accessory`, `test`, `cool_down`, or `class`.
- Put the purpose, the intended stimulus, and the scaling rule in `instructions`.
- Use `planned_reps`, `planned_weight_kg`, `planned_distance_m`, and
  `planned_duration_s` when a number is fixed. Each must be positive.
- `completion_status` starts as `planned`.

## Good and bad plans

A bad plan: "Monday: Fran. Tuesday: Murph. Wednesday: max snatch. Thursday: 100
muscle-ups." It has no purpose per session, ignores class, ignores the athlete's
level, and stacks pulling every day.

A good plan names the classes the athlete already attends, adds two short sessions
with one purpose each, keeps the hardest pulling away from the day after a
pulling-heavy class, and states the test that will show whether it worked.
