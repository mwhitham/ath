# Scaling

Scaling changes a workout so this athlete gets the effect the workout was written
for. Every scaling choice has a reason and a route back to the prescribed version.

## The rule

- **Official.** "When scaling workouts, the main principle to follow is 'preserve the
  stimulus.'" The stimulus is "the effects of the specific combination of
  movements, time domain, and load" (L1 p. 77).
- **Official.** Scaling means to "adhere to as many of the original workout factors
  as possible relative to the individual's physical and psychological tolerances"
  (L1 p. 83).
- **Official.** No single rule covers every case. The athlete's or a qualified
  trainer's judgment decides (L1 p. 77).
- **Official.** "Scaling should be viewed as a progressive tool to move athletes
  beyond their current level, not a crutch used to avoid difficulty" (CrossFit,
  2020).
- **Research.** No study tests whether a scaled workout gives the same adaptation
  as the prescribed one. "Preserve the stimulus" is CrossFit's guidance, not a
  tested rule.

## Words used here

- **Rx**: the workout as prescribed.
- **Intended stimulus**: what the workout should feel like and do. It has a time
  domain, an intensity, and a limiting factor.
- **Time domain**: how long the workout should take.
- **Limiting factor**: what should stop the athlete going faster. Usually breathing
  in short conditioning, and a muscle group in heavy work.
- **Unbroken**: a set done without putting the bar down or coming off the rig.

## Decision procedure

Run these steps in order. Write down the answer to each.

### Step 1. Name the intended stimulus

Find the time domain first.

1. Use the class coach's stated intent if there is one, such as "7 to 10 minutes".
2. Otherwise use the benchmark page or the workout's own words.
3. Otherwise use the time domains in [programming.md](programming.md): short under
   5 minutes, couplet 7 to 10, triplet 8 to 15, long 20 or more (O'Connor, 2023).

Then name the intensity and the limiting factor. "Fast, with sets broken at most
two or three times, limited by breathing" is a stimulus. "Hard" is not.

**Official.** Scaled times "should be relatively similar" to advanced athletes'
times. An advanced athlete may finish Fran in 2 minutes. A new athlete doing it as
prescribed may take 15 minutes or more. "Fran should be completed within several
minutes" (L1 p. 78). The Fran lesson plan says to finish "in less than 10 minutes"
(L1 p. 88). crossfit.com calls 10 to 12 minutes "a great goal for all athletes"
(crossfit.com Fran page). **Practice.** Treat about 10 minutes as the upper limit
for a scaled Fran.

### Step 2. Predict the athlete's result as written

Use the record:

- the athlete's times on similar workouts;
- their unbroken set sizes for each movement (from the assessment);
- their competence level for each movement (see [assessment.md](assessment.md)).

**Practice.** A useful check: total reps of a movement divided by the athlete's
repeatable set size under fatigue gives the number of sets. A fresh maximum is not
a repeatable set. Under fatigue, assume about half of the fresh maximum.

If the predicted time fits the window and every movement is at level 3 for that
load, do it as prescribed. Otherwise continue.

### Step 3. Change load first

- **Official.** "Load is the variable to scale first" (L1 p. 78).
- **Official.** In conditioning, choose a load that lets the athlete finish the
  first set or round with good form and without reaching muscular failure
  (L1 p. 78).
- **Official.** New athletes often use "less than 50 percent of the prescribed
  load". "Coaches should err on the side of scaling too much rather than not
  enough" (L1 p. 78).
- **Official.** For Fran, crossfit.com suggests a thruster load that allows "at
  least 15 unbroken reps" (crossfit.com Fran page).

For a body-weight movement, load means how much of the body the athlete lifts.
A band, a box, a lower ring, or a higher foot position reduces it.

### Step 4. Change volume next

- **Official.** Volume can be cut by reducing "1) time; 2) reps/rounds; and/or
  3) distance" (L1 p. 78).
- **Official.** Beginners should cut intensity and volume by about half for at
  least two weeks (L1 p. 78).
- **Official.** Example: Cindy, a 20-minute AMRAP (as many rounds and reps as
  possible), becomes a 10-minute AMRAP, or 10 rounds for time (L1 p. 80).

Keep the descending shape when you cut reps. For example, 21-15-9 becomes 15-12-9
(L1 p. 91) or 12-9-6 (the crossfit.com Fran intermediate pull-ups).

### Step 5. Change the movement last

- **Official.** Change the movement last, because avoiding a movement stops the
  athlete getting better at it. Reduce the load first: snatch with a PVC pipe
  rather than overhead squat at 95 lb (L1 p. 79).
- **Official.** Swap a movement only for a physical limit, an injury, or when the
  load cannot be reduced. Match the original on four points: upper or lower body;
  function, such as push or pull; range of motion, especially at the hips, knees,
  and ankles; and plane of movement (L1 p. 79).
- **Official.** Prefer a movement progression to a swap. Squat snatches can become
  snatch-grip deadlifts, hang power snatches, and overhead squats with a pipe
  (CrossFit, 2020).
- With an injury, use the clinician's guidance. Do not choose a swap to work around
  pain on your own judgment.

### Step 6. Write the reason and the route back

For each change, write:

1. what changed (load, volume, or movement);
2. why, linked to the stimulus;
3. the condition for stepping back toward Rx.

Use one route-back rule for each situation:

- **For a movement that comes up often in class (Practice).** Step back toward Rx
  when the athlete finishes two workouts in a row inside the intended window, holds
  the planned set sizes to the end, and keeps good positions.
- **For a benchmark done rarely, such as Fran (Official).** A scaled athlete who
  finished last time "under 5 minutes" gets a harder version next time (L1 p. 91).

Either way, change one thing at a time: a thinner band, a few more reps, or one
load step.

### Step 7. Check after the workout

- Did the time land in the window?
- Were sets broken as predicted?
- Did form hold? Ask the athlete or their class coach.

If the time was far outside the window, the scaling was wrong. Change it next time.
Do not blame effort first.

## During a workout

- **Official.** "At the appearance of unsafe form, the athlete or coach should end
  the workout or reduce the load to that which allows proper mechanics" (L1 p. 77).
- **Official.** Avoid "progressive scaling" with beginner and intermediate
  athletes: lowering the load again and again so an exhausted athlete never stops.
  Let them rest instead (L1 p. 152).
- **Official.** If a scaled version is easier than expected, reps, load, or
  distance "may be increased slightly in the later rounds" (CrossFit, 2020).

## How long to keep scaling

- **Official.** "A month is the minimum period for which significant scaling should
  be applied." Load scaling "might continue for months and years" (L1 pp. 77, 82).
  Months at scaled loads are normal "for even the best athletes" (L1 p. 152).
- **Research.** One prospective cohort linked switching back and forth between
  prescribed and scaled loads with more injuries (odds ratio 3.5, 95% confidence
  interval 1.7 to 7.3). This is limited evidence from a review of 3 cohorts
  (Mehrab et al., 2023). **Practice.** Set one scaling rule for a block. Do not pick
  Rx or scaled each day by mood.
- **Official.** Vary scaling choices across workouts so athletes do not avoid what
  challenges them most (CrossFit, 2020).
- **Practice.** How the two fit together: fix the rule for each movement for the
  whole block, such as "class pull-ups with the thin band". Vary which movements
  are scaled from workout to workout. Do not vary one movement's rule from day to
  day.

## Movement guides

Each ladder runs from closest to Rx to furthest. Use the first rung that fits the
stimulus. Labels apply to the whole row unless marked.

### Pull-ups

| Item | Guide |
|---|---|
| Check first | Strict pull-ups in one set. Kipping pull-ups in one set. Grip and shoulder comfort. |
| Ladder | 1. Rx reps, sets of 3 to 5 from the start. 2. Band-assisted pull-ups at the Rx reps, thinner band first (load). 3. Fewer reps, with or without the band: 21-15-9 becomes 12-9-6, as in the crossfit.com Fran intermediate (volume). 4. Jumping pull-ups. 5. Ring rows; walk the feet forward to make them harder. |
| Basis | Official: ring rows in the scaled Fran (L1 p. 85); strict before kipping (L1 p. 219); beginners should keep "negatives" (slow lowering) "to a minimum" because they are linked with rhabdomyolysis, and in a jumping pull-up should drop straight down rather than lower slowly (L1 p. 152). The order of rungs follows load, then volume, then movement (L1 pp. 78–79); the rungs themselves are Practice. |
| Stimulus note | In a short couplet the pull-ups should allow a few sets per round, not singles. Singles turn a 7-minute workout into a long grind. |
| Route back | Thinner band, then no band at reduced reps, then more reps. Strict work in separate skill sessions builds the base. |
| Caution | Research: wide-grip and reverse-grip strict pull-ups showed shoulder movement linked with impingement risk in 11 people (Prinold and Bull, 2016). Practice: use about shoulder-width grip when learning. |

### Toes-to-bar

| Item | Guide |
|---|---|
| Check first | Can the athlete hang, swing with control, and lift the knees? Is grip or the trunk the limit? |
| Ladder | 1. Rx reps in small sets. 2. Fewer reps. 3. Knees-to-chest or hanging knee raises. 4. Kip swings plus V-ups or sit-ups, for an athlete who cannot hang for long. |
| Basis | Practice. |
| Stimulus note | Rung 4 removes the grip demand. Say so, because a workout with pull-ups and toes-to-bar loads grip twice. |
| Route back | Knees higher each week, then single toes-to-bar, then small sets. |

### Double-unders

| Item | Guide |
|---|---|
| Check first | Double-unders in one set, and how often the rope trips the athlete. |
| Ladder | 1. Rx reps. 2. Fewer double-unders. 3. A fixed time of double-under attempts, such as 30 or 60 seconds. 4. Single-unders, often about twice the reps. |
| Basis | Practice. No official ratio exists. |
| Stimulus note | Choose the rung that keeps the athlete moving. Long tripping spells change the workout more than single-unders do. |
| Caution | Research: with fatigue, athletes used more shoulder internal rotation (Bruce et al., 2017; 10 athletes). Practise the skill while fresh. |
| Route back | Short practice sets while fresh, outside conditioning. Then use a reduced number in workouts. |

### Handstand push-ups

| Item | Guide |
|---|---|
| Check first | Can the athlete kick up to a wall, hold, and come down safely? Strict handstand push-ups in one set? Any neck symptoms? |
| Ladder | 1. Rx reps, small sets. 2. Box pike handstand push-ups, feet on a box, so the hands carry less body weight (load). 3. Fewer reps (volume). 4. Pike push-ups. 5. Dumbbell or barbell press. |
| Basis | Official: in the scaled Diane, handstand push-ups become a 10-lb dumbbell press (L1 p. 85). The other rungs are Practice. |
| Stimulus note | A press keeps the pushing pattern and the shoulder range. It removes being upside down. |
| Caution | Neck pain, tingling, or numbness goes to a clinician. |
| Route back | Strict handstand push-ups before kipping ones (Practice). |

### Muscle-ups

| Item | Guide |
|---|---|
| Check first | Strict pull-ups, chest-to-bar pull-ups, and ring or bar dips in one set. |
| Ladder | 1. Rx reps, singles. 2. Banded or low-ring transitions (load). 3. Fewer reps (volume). 4. Chest-to-bar pull-ups plus ring or bar dips. 5. Pull-ups plus dips. |
| Basis | Official: a strict muscle-up before kipping muscle-ups (L1 p. 232). Faults the guide names: "Losing the false grip", a body too upright in the pull, flared elbows, and "Not pulling the rings low enough before beginning the transition" (L1 pp. 236–239). The ladder is Practice. |
| Stimulus note | A common class rule is 2 to 3 pull-ups and 2 to 3 dips for each muscle-up. That is Practice, not an official ratio. Check that the time domain still holds. |
| Route back | Strict pulling and dip strength, then strict transition work in skill sessions. |

### Heavy barbell work

| Item | Guide |
|---|---|
| Check first | Recent heavy sets for the lift, and the competence level. |
| In conditioning | Load that allows the first round unbroken, or the prescribed set size, with good positions (L1 p. 78). |
| In a heavy session | Build to a heavy set with good mechanics. Stop the set when form changes. |
| Olympic lifts | Official: snatch needs an overhead squat, which needs an air squat (L1 p. 159). Scale through a progression: pipe or empty bar, hang power versions, then full lifts (CrossFit, 2020). |
| Deadlift | Official: a lost lower-back curve means abort and lower the load (L1 pp. 195–200). |
| Route back | Add one load step when the athlete keeps positions and finishes in the window twice. |

## Worked example: Fran in a class

**Official** class scaling for Fran (L1 p. 91):

- Rx: 21-15-9 thrusters at 95 lb (43 kg) for men, 65 lb (29 kg) for women, and
  pull-ups.
- Intermediate: thrusters at 95/65 lb, pull-ups 15-12-9.
- Beginner: thrusters at 65/45 lb, ring rows.
- Target: under 10 minutes. A scaled athlete who finished last time "under 5
  minutes" gets a harder version. Suggested breaks: no more than three in the 21s,
  two in the 15s, and one in the 9s (L1 pp. 88, 91).

Applied to a woman whose record shows these numbers:

- **Observed:** thrusters, 8 unbroken at 29 kg and 16 unbroken at 20 kg (two class
  records). Pull-ups, 4 kipping unbroken, and 8 unbroken with the medium band. All
  fresh.

Step 1. **Stimulus:** a few minutes, under 10; at most three breaks in the 21s, two
in the 15s, and one in the 9s (L1 pp. 88, 91).

Step 2. **Prediction as written** (Practice: about half the fresh maximum per set
under fatigue):

- Thrusters at 29 kg: about 4 a set, so 5 or 6 sets for the 21s. Too many breaks.
- Kipping pull-ups: about 2 a set, so 22 or 23 sets for 45 reps. Far past 10 minutes.

Step 3. **Load first:**

- Thrusters at 20 kg. She has done 16 unbroken, which meets crossfit.com's "at least
  15 unbroken reps". About 8 a set under fatigue gives 3 sets for the 21s: two
  breaks.
- Pull-ups with the medium band. About 4 a set under fatigue gives 6 sets for the
  21s. Still too many breaks.

Step 4. **Volume next:** pull-ups 12-9-6, banded, the crossfit.com Fran intermediate
rep scheme. That is 3 sets, 3 sets, and 2 sets: two, two, and one break.
**Hypothesis:** this keeps more pull in each rep than a thicker band would, and she
can already do 8 fresh with the medium band.

Step 5. **Movement:** no change. Not ring rows, because she can do pull-ups and
should practise them (L1 p. 79).

Step 6. **Route back:** Fran is done rarely, so use the guide's rule. When this
version is finished under 5 minutes, make the next Fran harder by one step
(L1 p. 91). Steps, one at a time: pull-ups 15-12-9 with the band; then a thinner
band; then thrusters at 25 kg; then the next step toward 29 kg and 21-15-9.

Step 7. **Next check:** finish under 10 minutes, with no more breaks than the guide's
split.

## Good and bad scaling answers

Bad: "Just do ring rows." It gives no reason, ignores the athlete's pull-ups, and
gives no way back.

Good: "Use 20 kg for the thrusters, because you have done 16 unbroken at that load.
Use the medium band and 12-9-6 pull-ups. Fran is meant to take a few minutes. At
29 kg and 45 pull-ups without a band, you would break both movements into many
small sets and it would take far longer. When you finish this version under 5
minutes, we make it harder by one step."
