# What the evidence says

Read this file before you tell an athlete what research shows, and when you need the
strength of a claim. Full citations and links are in [sources.md](sources.md).

Every study here is summarised in one of two research notes kept with the Athletic
Standard repository (see [sources.md](sources.md#research-notes-behind-this-skill)):
the HYROX research notes ("HRN", with a section number) and the calculation-methods
notes ("CMN"). A claim without a study is coaching practice and is labelled so.

## Labels

| Label | Meaning | How to say it to the athlete |
|---|---|---|
| **Research** | A peer-reviewed HYROX finding. The entry gives the sample and limits. | "A study of 24 PRO men found…" |
| **Transferable** | Peer-reviewed research from another sport, applied to HYROX by reasoning. | "In runners (not HYROX athletes), strength training improved…" |
| **Practice** | Common coaching practice. No HYROX study tests it. | "A common approach is…" |
| **Unverified** | A preprint, a summary, an inference, or a weak report. A lead, not a fact. | "One unreviewed analysis suggests…" |
| **Hypothesis** | The coach's judgment for this athlete, to be tested on their data. | "My working guess for you is… We will test it by…" |

No good-quality controlled training trial tests any HYROX training plan (HRN 7).
Every training method in this skill is transferable evidence or coaching practice.
Say so when an athlete asks "what does the science say is best".

## HYROX studies

### What makes up race time (HRN 3, 4.1, 4.2)

- **Research.** Running was about 48–51% of total time in Top 100 PRO results, in
  both sexes, across seven seasons (Rappelt et al. 2026; about 39,700 results).
  Running rank tracked overall rank more closely than any station did. Limits:
  public data, PRO only, Roxzone layouts vary.
- **Research.** Strength stations cost slower athletes much more time than faster
  athletes (Rappelt et al. 2026).
- **Research.** In 11,592 male PRO results, running plus Roxzone and the stations
  each explained about half of the differences between athletes. Wall balls, sled
  pull, and Roxzone were the largest single parts. "Strong runner" and "strong
  station" profiles reached similar times (Yustres Amores et al. 2026). Limits: men,
  PRO, observational.
- **Research.** In one lab simulation, 11 recreational athletes (3 women) finished
  in a median 86.5 min: runs 51.2 min, stations 32.8 min. Higher VO2max, more
  endurance training, and lower body fat went with faster times. Grip strength and
  resistance-training volume did not (Brandt et al. 2025). Limits: 11 people,
  treadmill runs, a non-standard sled, no crowd, lower-body strength not measured.
  **Unverified (arithmetic in HRN 4.1):** runs were about 59% of time in this slower
  sample.

What this means: running fitness matters for nearly everyone, and stations matter
more the slower the athlete is. "Train running first" is reasonable practice, but it
is not a rule for every athlete (HRN 4.2). Look at the athlete's own splits.

### How pace changes during the race (HRN 4.3)

"Compromised running" is a coaching term for running while tired from a station. It
is not a defined scientific measure.

- **Research.** In 24 PRO men at one official race, run speed fell from 16.1 km/h
  in run 1 to 13.6 km/h in run 8. Mean running speed was 84% of the speed at the
  second ventilatory threshold (Gutiérrez-Hellín et al. 2026, *IJSPP*). Limits: men,
  one race, abstract only.
- **Research.** Run 8 shows the clearest slowing. Late runs are only partly
  predictable from early-race splits (Li et al. 2026; 61,541 records).
- **Research.** In top-ranked results, neither split variability nor late-race fade
  was clearly linked to finish time once event and repeat athletes were handled
  (Shi et al. 2026; 1,121 results). This does not show that pacing is unimportant
  for one athlete.
- **Research.** In the lab simulation, run 5 (after burpee broad jumps) was the
  slowest run, then runs 6 and 8 (Brandt et al. 2025, n = 11).
- **Unverified (preprint).** The run after sandbag lunges and the run after the sled
  push slowed most; the run after the row slowed least (Zhu et al. 2026; 13,618
  results).

What this means: some slowing across the race is normal. A large fade alone does not
prove poor fitness or poor pacing. Check the athlete's own pattern.

### Health and injury (HRN 6)

- **Research.** In 89 HYROX athletes followed for 12 weeks, 30% reported a health
  problem in a typical week. The injury rate was 14.4 per 1,000 hours, 73% were
  overuse injuries, and the knee was most common (Chittenden et al. 2026). Limits:
  62% response rate, self-report.
- **Unverified (preprint).** In an online survey of 418 athletes, more HYROX-specific
  sessions per week went with more injury reports (Ketzer et al. 2026). The design
  cannot show cause.
- **Research.** Eight male HYROX athletes slept about 6.6 h. They took longer to fall
  asleep after resistance sessions than after endurance sessions (Buoite Stella et
  al. 2026). Very small sample.
- **Practice.** Because overuse knee and lower-leg problems are common, coaches raise
  running load gradually and rotate lunge and sled volume. No HYROX study tests a
  prevention program, and no study gives a safe weekly increase.

### Benchmarks (HRN 3.2)

- **Research.** Percentile tables for every segment exist, up to the 2023/24 season
  (Fernández-Navarrete et al. 2026; 186,411 results). The research notes read the
  abstract only.
- **Unverified (inference in HRN 3.2).** PRO times kept falling after 2023/24, so
  these percentiles may now flatter an athlete.
- Use them only to locate a weak segment after a race, never as a race target.

## Transferable evidence (HRN 5)

None of these studies tested HYROX athletes.

### Strength and endurance in one program (HRN 5.1)

- **Transferable.** Wilson et al. 2012 (meta-analysis, 21 studies): strength gains
  were smaller with added endurance work (effect size 1.44 against 1.76 for strength
  alone), and power gains more so (0.55 against 0.91). Running, but not cycling,
  reduced gains. A later meta-analysis did not confirm the running-versus-cycling
  finding.
- **Transferable.** Schumann et al. 2022 (43 studies): no loss in maximal strength
  or muscle size. Explosive strength was reduced (SMD −0.28). The loss appeared when
  both were in the same session and was not significant when sessions were at least
  3 hours apart.
- **Transferable.** Petré et al. 2021 (27 studies): trained people lost some
  lower-body strength gain, mainly when both were in the same session.
- **Transferable.** Huiberts et al. 2024 (59 studies): lower-body strength gains were
  blunted in men but not in women. Few women and few highly trained athletes were
  studied.
- **Transferable.** Order in one session: strength first gave slightly more
  lower-body strength and no difference in VO2max (Murlasits et al. 2018; Eddens et
  al. 2018; Gao and Yu 2023).
- **Practice (HRN 5.1).** When leg strength or power matters, put hard leg work and
  hard running on different days, or at least 3 hours apart. In a shared session,
  lift first.

### Strength training for running (HRN 5.2)

- **Transferable.** Blagrove et al. 2018 (24 studies of runners): running economy
  generally improved by 2–8% against running-only groups, and 1.5–10 km time trials
  tended to improve.
- **Transferable.** Balsalobre-Fernández et al. 2016 (5 studies, 93 highly trained
  runners): a large improvement in running economy from programs 2–3 times a week
  for 8–12 weeks. Only 5 small studies.
- **Transferable.** Llanos-Lagos et al. 2024 (31 studies, 652 runners): heavy loads
  (at least 80% of the one-repetition maximum) and combined methods improved running
  economy. Jump training helped only at slower speeds.
- **Transferable.** Zanini et al. 2025 (28 well-trained men, 10 weeks): strength and
  jump training twice a week kept running economy better late in a 90-minute run.
- **Practice (HRN 5.2).** Heavy lifting plus jumps, 2–3 times a week, to support the
  running part of HYROX.

Running economy means the oxygen cost of running at a set speed. Lower is better.

### Running intensity (HRN 5.3)

- **Transferable.** No single split of easy and hard running was clearly best for
  race performance across recent meta-analyses (Silva Oliveira et al. 2024;
  Rosenblat et al. 2025).
- **Transferable.** Different threshold tests give different numbers (Faude et al.
  2009; Galán-Rioja et al. 2020). Use the same test each time.
- **Practice.** Keep most running easy and add some hard sessions.

### Running after other work (HRN 5.4)

- **Transferable.** In triathlon, the energy cost of running after a 40 km ride was
  1.6% to 11.6% higher than fresh running, depending on athlete level (Millet and
  Vleck 2000, review).
- **Transferable.** In 12 triathletes over 6 weeks, repeated short ride-run blocks
  did not improve overall ride-run performance more than normal training, but did
  speed up the transition and the first 333 m (Hue et al. 2002).
- **Practice.** "Brick" sessions: a station, then a run, repeated.
- Not shown: that station-then-run training improves total HYROX time. The earlier
  work in these studies was cycling, not sleds or lunges.

### Sleds (HRN 5.6)

- **Transferable.** Floor friction changed how much added weight slowed athletes
  towing a sled (Linthorne and Cooper 2013, 6 rugby players, 4 surfaces).
- **Transferable.** The load that slowed a sled push by half ranged from 45% to 85% of
  body mass in 90 young male athletes (Cahill et al. 2021). The same sled load is a
  different stimulus for different people.
- Not shown: what training best improves a heavy 50 m sled push or pull.

### Squat depth under fatigue (HRN 5.7)

- **Transferable.** After a very hard short-rest workout, 12 trained men squatted
  less deeply (Hooper et al. 2013). Watch wall-ball depth late in training and racing.
- No study of wall-ball technique under fatigue exists.

### Pacing (HRN 5.8)

- **Transferable.** A review found that, for events longer than about 2 minutes,
  fairly even pacing may give the best times (Abbiss and Laursen 2008).
- **Transferable.** In 13 triathletes, a fast first km of the run raised oxygen use,
  lactate, heart rate, and effort in that km (Skroce et al. 2022).
- **Transferable.** In 13 male CrossFit competitors doing the same workout twice, a
  controlled start gave round scores of 99, 98, and 97; an all-out start gave 114,
  80, and 80 (Ribeiro et al. 2024).
- **Practice (HRN 5.8, 8).** A controlled run 1, a little slower than goal pace, a
  steady middle race, and what is left spent on the last run and wall balls.
- No HYROX pacing trial exists.

### Tapering (HRN 5.5)

- **Transferable.** Bosquet et al. 2007 (27 studies): the best taper lasted 2 weeks,
  cut volume by 41–60% in a falling curve, and kept intensity and frequency the
  same. There was no control group.
- **Transferable.** Wang et al. 2023 (14 studies of endurance athletes): time-trial
  performance improved after a taper. Effective tapers cut volume by 41–60%, kept
  intensity and frequency, and lasted 21 days or less.
- **Practice.** HYROX coaches commonly cut volume for about 1–2 weeks, keep some
  race-pace work, and keep some station practice.
- No HYROX taper study exists.

## Monitoring evidence (CMN 1–8)

These are the athlete-monitoring rules the calculation skills use.

- **Research.** Weekly means of morning HRV (Ln RMSSD: the natural log of RMSSD, a
  common HRV statistic) tracked fitness change far better than single days (Plews et
  al. 2013, 10 runners). At least 3 valid readings a week are needed (Plews et al.
  2014). A band of ±0.5 standard deviations around the athlete's own mean is a common
  "smallest worthwhile change" (Vesterinen et al. 2016, 40 runners). Limits: small
  samples, morning chest-strap recordings, not overnight wrist data.
- RMSSD and SDNN are different statistics. Never compare them or put them in one
  baseline (CMN 1).
- **Research.** Resting heart rate is also more useful as a weekly mean than as one
  day (Plews et al. 2013).
- **Research.** Session RPE (a rating of the whole session times its minutes) is a
  valid group-level load measure in many sports (Foster et al. 2001; Haddad et al.
  2017) and in high-intensity functional training (Crawford et al. 2018, 25 adults).
  In Crawford, matching a single rating to heart-rate intensity was poor (ICC 0.583).
  Compare loads only when they share the same scale, the same duration rule, and the
  same delay after the session (CMN 8).
- **Research.** Age formulas for maximum heart rate have an error of about 11 beats
  per minute for one person (Nes et al. 2013, 3,320 adults). Use a measured maximum
  for zones.
- **Practice.** Do not use an acute-to-chronic load ratio, or any load threshold, to
  predict injury. No HYROX study supports one.

## What the evidence does not establish (HRN 7)

Say these plainly when they matter:

- No validated split of running, strength, and station work for HYROX.
- No HYROX pacing, taper, nutrition, or heat study.
- No injury-prevention trial in HYROX.
- No physiology study of Doubles, Relay, or age groups.
- No test battery shown to predict one athlete's HYROX time.
- Few women in HYROX physiology studies.
- Correlation is not cause. A higher VO2max goes with faster times, but no study
  shows how much raising it lowers HYROX time.
- Split data are messy: venues differ, Roxzone layouts differ, and rules changed
  across seasons. Compare splits only within the same event when possible.
