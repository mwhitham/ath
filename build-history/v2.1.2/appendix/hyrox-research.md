# HYROX coaching: what the evidence says and does not say

> Compiled on 2026-11-15 by a research agent for the `hyrox` coaching skill (D228). "I" in the text means
> that compiler. It is research input, not a decision; the decisions are in
> [../decisions.md](../decisions.md). Sources were read on that date.

Search date: 15 November 2026. Searches covered PubMed, Europe PMC, Crossref, Frontiers, MDPI, BMC, Springer, and general web search.

This file feeds a coaching skill. It keeps three kinds of claims apart. Each claim carries one label.

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[Research]** | A finding reported in a peer-reviewed study. The tag adds the study and its size, so the reader can judge its weight. |
| **[Practice]** | Expert or coaching practice. It may be sensible, but no HYROX study tests it. Some of it rests on research from other sports, which is named. |
| **[Unverified]** | A preprint, a news summary, a blog, a vendor analysis, or my own arithmetic on published numbers. Treat it as a lead, not a fact. |

A coach turns research and practice into a hypothesis about one athlete. Section 8 shows how. Anything about one athlete is a hypothesis until that athlete's own data supports it.

## 1. Short summary

- **[Research]** HYROX is mainly an endurance event. Running is the largest single block of race time. In the Top 100 PRO results it is about 48 to 51% of total time (Rappelt et al. 2026, about 39,700 results).
- **[Research]** In the one lab study, a higher VO2max, more endurance training, and lower body fat went with faster finish times. Grip strength and resistance-training volume did not (Brandt et al. 2025, n = 11).
- **[Research]** Stations still matter. In 11,592 male PRO results, running plus Roxzone and the stations each explained about half of the differences between athletes. Wall balls, sled pull, and Roxzone were the largest single parts (Yustres Amores et al. 2026).
- **[Research]** Strength stations cost slower athletes much more time than faster athletes (Rappelt et al. 2026).
- **[Research]** Run pace drops across the race. In 24 PRO men, speed fell from about 16.1 km/h in run 1 to about 13.6 km/h in run 8 (Gutiérrez-Hellín et al. 2026).
- **[Research]** In top-ranked results, the size of late-race slowing was not clearly linked to finish time once the analysis allowed for event and athlete (Shi et al. 2026).
- **[Research]** Health problems are common. One 12-week study found an injury rate of 14.4 per 1,000 hours. Most were overuse injuries, mainly at the knee and lower leg (Chittenden et al. 2026, n = 89).
- No controlled training trial of good quality tests any HYROX training plan. Every training method in this file is borrowed from other sports or is coaching practice.

### 3.2 Study summaries

#### Brandt et al. 2025 — the only lab study of a full simulated race

- **Citation:** Brandt T, Ebel C, Lebahn C, Schmidt A. Acute physiological responses and performance determinants in Hyrox© – a new running-focused high intensity functional fitness trend. *Front Physiol.* 2025;16:1519240. https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2025.1519240 (PMC: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11994925/)
- **Design:** Lab tests (body composition, grip strength, treadmill VO2max test). Then one simulated Open-division race, 2–14 days later. Heart rate was recorded throughout. Lactate and RPE were taken after each run and each station.
- **Sample:** 11 recreational athletes, 3 women and 8 men. Median age 33. Median VO2max 51 mL/kg/min (range 46.6–72). Median HYROX experience 18 months.
- **Findings [Research, n = 11]:**
  - Median finish time 86.5 min. Runs took 51.2 min. Stations took 32.8 min (p = 0.003).
  - About 79.5% of race time was spent at "very hard" heart rate intensity and 19.6% at "hard".
  - Mean heart rate was 170.9 bpm. It was slightly higher during stations (173.7) than runs (168.9).
  - Mean lactate was 6.3 mmol/L. Peak lactate was 8.5 mmol/L. Stations gave slightly higher lactate than runs.
  - The final station (wall balls) gave peak values: heart rate 183 bpm, lactate 8.5 mmol/L, RPE 18.
  - Correlations with finish time (Spearman): VO2max ρ = −0.71 (p = 0.01). Endurance training volume ρ = −0.68 (p = 0.04). Body fat ρ = 0.67 (p = 0.03).
  - No clear link with grip strength (ρ = 0.09), resistance training volume (ρ = 0.34, p = 0.31), body mass, or muscle mass.
  - Run 5 was the slowest run (median 7.4 min). Runs 6 and 8 were next (6.8 min each).
- **Limitations:** Very small sample. Runs were on a treadmill. The sled was not the official sled. Athletes raced alone, without a crowd or rivals. Only grip strength was tested, so lower-body strength was not measured. The paper has small inconsistencies between its text and tables (for example, the sign of one correlation).
- **Note:** The 2025 scoping review (Villarroel López & Juárez Santos-García) says resistance training volume correlated with performance in this study. The Brandt data do not show that (ρ = 0.34, p = 0.31). Cite Brandt directly, not the review.

#### Rappelt et al. 2026 — seven seasons of PRO and ELITE results

- **Citation:** Rappelt L, Wiedenmann T, Held S, Heinke L, Micke F, Wicker P, Donath L. Longitudinal performance development in PRO and ELITE HYROX competitions across the first seven competitive seasons. *Front Physiol.* 2026;17:1847569. https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2026.1847569
- **Design:** All PRO and ELITE individual results from results.hyrox.com, seasons 1–7 (2018/19 to 2024/25). Percentile curves, rank comparisons, quantile regression, and mixed models.
- **Sample:** 27,854 male results (236 ELITE) and 11,842 female results (237 ELITE), after removing duplicates, penalties, and missing splits.
- **Findings [Research, large public dataset]:**
  - Top 100 total time fell from 70:13 to 56:56 in men (about 19%) and from 80:43 to 63:11 in women (about 21%).
  - Running stayed at about 48–51% of total time in both sexes across seasons.
  - Running rank matched overall rank more closely than any station did.
  - Strength stations improved the most over the seasons (for example, men's wall balls 6:06 to 3:55). SkiErg, row, and burpee broad jumps changed little.
  - Quantile regression: each extra unit of station time cost slower athletes more total time than faster athletes. The effect was largest for strength stations.
  - The ELITE field converged. The spread of men's ELITE times (coefficient of variation) fell from 10.4% to 4.7%.
- **Limitations stated by the authors:** Public data, partly self-reported. PRO has no qualifying standard. Roxzone layouts vary, so pacing could not be studied in detail. Running gains may partly reflect better pacing, not fitness. No physiological measures. Season 3 was very small because of COVID-19.

#### Gutiérrez-Hellín et al. 2026 (HYScience) — run intensity in a real race

- **Citation:** Gutiérrez-Hellín J, Alonso-Domínguez S, Gil-Cabrera J, et al. Relative Running Intensity and Pacing During an Official HYROX PRO MEN Competition: The HYScience Study. *Int J Sports Physiol Perform.* 2026 (advance online), 1–8. https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2026-0098
- **Design:** Treadmill test, then the official HYROX Madrid 2025 race within 10 days. Run speeds came from official splits and were compared with each athlete's lab thresholds.
- **Sample:** 24 PRO men. Mean race time 69:58 (SD 5:05). Mean VO2max 51.6 mL/kg/min. VT2 speed 16.4 km/h. Speed at VO2max 18.1 km/h.
- **Findings [Research, n = 24]:**
  - Run speed fell from 16.13 km/h in run 1 to 13.58 km/h in run 8.
  - Roxzone was the slowest part, at 10.52 km/h.
  - Across all runs and Roxzone, mean speed was 84% of VT2 speed and 76% of the speed at VO2max.
  - The authors conclude that holding a higher relative intensity appears central to a faster total run time.
- **[Unverified, my arithmetic]** Run 1 was about 98% of VT2 speed (16.13 / 16.41). Run 8 was about 83%. This suggests these men started near threshold and faded.
- **Limitations:** Men only, one race, one division. Only the abstract was available to me. Heart rate and lactate during the race were not reported in the abstract.

#### Gutiérrez-Hellín et al. 2026 — seven seasons of the Top 50 PRO men

- **Citation:** Gutiérrez-Hellín J, Alonso-Domínguez S, Martín-Gómez FJ, Muñoz A, Franco-Andrés A, Yustres-Amores I, del Cerro JS, Del Coso J. Performance Trends and Pacing Evolution in Elite HYROX©: A Seven-Season Analysis of Top 50 PRO Men (2018–2024). *Appl Sci.* 2026;16(13):6674. https://doi.org/10.3390/app16136674
- **Design:** Top 50 PRO men each season from the official database. 350 results from 138 athletes.
- **Findings [Research, top men only]:**
  - Total time fell 20.8% (−891.5 s) from 2018 to 2024.
  - Run plus Roxzone time fell 21.8%. Station time fell 19.2%.
  - Wall balls (−34.6%) and sled pull (−29.3%) improved most.
  - Run 8 improved most of the runs (−19.2%, −58.5 s).
  - Run profiles became more even over time, but late-race slowing persisted.
- **Limitations:** Men only. Top 50 only. Descriptive. The authors note the data show changing standards, not the same athletes improving (only 46 of 138 athletes appear in more than one season). **[Unverified, my note]** Venues and equipment may also differ between seasons.

#### Yustres Amores et al. 2026 — how race segments relate to total time

- **Citation:** Yustres Amores I, Gutiérrez-Hellín J, Alonso-Domínguez S, Muñoz A, Franco-Andrés A, Del Coso J, del Cerro JS. Performance Architecture and Competitive Phenotypes in HYROX: A Large-Scale Analysis of Official Race Performances. *Appl Sci.* 2026;16(21):10832. https://doi.org/10.3390/app162110832
- **Design:** One result per athlete. Segment times were centred within each event to remove venue effects. Partial correlations, variance breakdown, and clustering in the fastest quarter.
- **Sample:** 11,592 male PRO athletes, 143 events, 2018–2025.
- **Findings [Research, men only]:**
  - Every segment correlated with total time (partial r = 0.55 to 0.78).
  - Running plus Roxzone explained about half of between-athlete variance. Stations explained the other half.
  - Wall balls, sled pull, and Roxzone were the largest single parts.
  - Mid-race runs were most strongly linked to the rest of the race.
  - In the fastest quarter, two profiles appeared: relatively faster stations, or relatively faster running. Both reached similar total times. The profiles overlapped heavily (silhouette 0.18). They did not tell faster from slower athletes (AUC about 0.54).
- **Limitations:** Men only. PRO only. Observational. The profiles are points on a continuum, not distinct athlete types.

#### Olivares-Llorente et al. 2026 — pacing by sex and level

- **Citation:** Olivares-Llorente R, Martín-Castellanos A, Agudo-Ortega A, Martín-Castellanos C, Barba-Ruiz M, Hermosilla-Perona F. Pacing and effort distribution in Hyrox® PRO athletes: differences by sex and performance level. *BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil.* 2026. https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-026-02155-0
- **Access:** The paper is open access, but I could not load it. The details below come from a news summary (bioengineer.org, 12 November 2026).
- **Findings [Unverified, from a news summary]:**
  - 6,081 men and 2,341 women in PRO.
  - The shape of run pacing was very similar across performance groups. Small differences appeared at km 1, 2, and 8.
  - The effect of sex on run pacing was trivial (partial eta squared about 0.005).
  - Slower athletes lost more time on loaded and bodyweight stations. Ergometer stations made up a smaller share of station time as performance fell.
  - Women spent a larger share of time on SkiErg, burpee broad jumps, row, and farmers carry. Men spent a larger share on sandbag lunges and wall balls.
- **Limitations:** Observational. Event and season not stated in the summary. Sex differences may reflect the different PRO loads for men and women.

#### Shi et al. 2026 — does late-race fade predict finish time?

- **Citation:** Shi H, Zhong J, Xu J, Lu C. Structural screening and event-aware analysis of official HYROX split data: a retrospective study of top-ranked results. *BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil.* 2026. https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-026-02187-6
- **Design:** 2,000 top-ranked results from four ranking groups, archived on 29 March 2026. The main model controlled for event and for athletes who appear more than once.
- **Sample:** 1,121 results from 682 athletes across 116 event groups.
- **Findings [Research, top-ranked athletes only]:**
  - Neither run-split variability nor late-race run fade was clearly linked to finish time (both adjusted p = 0.364).
  - The answer changed with how repeat athletes were handled. Keeping each athlete's best result produced a significant fade effect. Neutral rules did not.
  - Over 500 random selections, the fade effect was positive 99.8% of the time, but its confidence interval excluded zero only 9% of the time.
  - There was no sign of a stable pacing trait within athletes.
- **Limitations:** Top-ranked results only. Observational. Screening rules were set by the authors.
- **Coaching meaning:** Among good athletes, how much they fade late does not clearly predict their time. That does not show that pacing is unimportant for an individual.

#### Li et al. 2026 — late-run slowing as a monitoring tool

- **Citation:** Li K, Ma K, Shao H, Xu Z. Observed pacing patterns and residual late-run variability in standardized hybrid fitness racing: A split-record framework for post-race performance monitoring in HYROX. *Int J Sports Sci Coach.* 2026. https://doi.org/10.1177/17479541261500529
- **Design:** Public split records. Early race data (runs 1–3 and stations 1–3) were used to predict runs 6–8.
- **Sample:** 61,541 results from 89 events (2021–2023). A second set of 19,769 results from 237 events was used to check the direction of results.
- **Findings [Research]:**
  - Run 8 shows the clearest slowing at the end of the race.
  - Late runs are only partly predictable from early-race data. The spread between the 90th and 10th percentile of unexplained late-run time was 67.8 s in the main sample.
  - Adding more race history shrinks the unexplained part, but does not remove it.
- **Authors' caution:** The measure should not be read as a fatigue diagnosis or a training threshold.

#### Zhu et al. 2026 — which station slows the next run most (preprint)

- **Citation:** Zhu Z, Zhu J, Li K, Shao HT, Ma K. Task-to-run transition stability in standardized hybrid fitness racing: a field-based performance analysis using real-world competition split records. Research Square preprint, 2026. https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-9772566/v1
- **Status:** Not peer reviewed. Shares authors with Li et al. 2026.
- **Sample:** 13,618 results, 132 races, 2024–2025. 95,326 station-to-run transitions.
- **Findings [Unverified, preprint]:**
  - Median change in run time compared with the previous run: run 8 after sandbag lunges +20 s, run 3 after sled push +14 s.
  - Run 6 after the row was the only run faster than the previous run (median −4 s).
  - After adjustment, runs after the row and the farmers carry were faster than expected. Runs after lunges and sled push were slower than expected.
  - The pattern held across sex, year, and PRO-only checks.
- **Meaning:** Slowing is not a simple straight line. Leg-heavy stations seem to hurt the next run most.

#### Fernández-Navarrete et al. 2026 — benchmark tables

- **Citation:** Fernández-Navarrete I, Ruiz-Alias SA, García-Pinillos F. Where am I struggling? Detailed benchmarks for every workout and category in Hyrox© competition. *J Sports Med Phys Fitness.* 2026;66:1087–1094. https://doi.org/10.23736/S0022-4707.26.17926-2
- **Design and sample:** 186,411 race results from the official ranking database, up to the 2023/24 season.
- **Findings [Research, descriptive]:** Mean and 10th–90th percentile times for each segment in individual, doubles, and relay categories. Intended to locate an athlete's weak segments.
- **Limitations:** Descriptive only. Data stop at 2023/24. I did not see the tables themselves. **[Unverified, my inference]** PRO times kept falling after that season (Rappelt et al. 2026), so these percentiles may now flatter an athlete.

#### Schwalm et al. 2026 — case study of a world champion

- **Citation:** Schwalm L, Schaffarczyk M, Mai J, Hollander K, Gronwald T, Fohrmann D. Physiological and biomechanical characteristics of a Hyrox world champion—a case study. *Ger J Exerc Sport Res.* 2026. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12662-026-01162-0
- **Design:** Step test and ramp test before and after a part-race simulation (5 × 1 km runs with row, farmers carry, lunges, and burpee broad jumps).
- **Athlete:** One man, 25 years, 80.7 kg. PRO single best 56:19. PRO doubles best 50:24.
- **Findings [Research, n = 1]:**
  - VO2max 61.3 mL/kg/min before and 57.0 after the simulation.
  - After the simulation he tended to show better running economy and lower lactate at higher speeds.
  - His step rate during runs seemed to change after certain stations.
  - The authors judged his running economy to be even more notable than his VO2max.
- **Limitations:** One athlete. Shortened race. Cannot be generalised.

#### Davids 2025 — narrative review

- **Citation:** Davids CJ. A Performance Analysis of HYROX: A Review of the Physiologic, Mechanical, and Technical Demands. *Strength Cond J.* 2026;48(1):88–100 (online May 2025). https://doi.org/10.1519/SSC.0000000000000913
- **Content [Practice, expert review]:** Names aerobic capacity as the base. Also names anaerobic power, local muscular endurance, and maximal strength. Suggests interval training, circuit training, and blood flow restriction training. States that HYROX-specific research is limited.
- **Limitation:** Published before most HYROX data existed. It reasons mostly from other sports.

#### Other HYROX papers

- **Villarroel López & Juárez Santos-García 2025, scoping review.** 39 studies on hybrid and functional fitness competition. Only one (Brandt) was HYROX-specific. It calls for trials. It misreports some Brandt results (see above). https://doi.org/10.3390/jfmk10040365
- **Villarroel López et al. 2025, athlete survey.** 80 athletes. Mostly men from functional or endurance backgrounds. Recovery practice was often unstructured. **[Research, self-report]** https://doi.org/10.3390/app152111693
- **Tibana et al. 2026, narrative review (preprint).** 11 studies. Running capacity was the most consistent determinant. Functional stations gave high lactate. Psychology, sleep, recovery, and injury are under-studied. **[Unverified, preprint]** https://doi.org/10.20944/preprints202607.1912.v1
- **Rios & Pyne 2025, narrative review.** Suggests low-cost load monitoring for daily coaching: heart rate, session RPE, and jump tests. Advanced tools are for research. **[Practice, expert review]** https://doi.org/10.3390/sports13110381
- **Buoite Stella et al. 2026.** 8 male HYROX athletes. Average sleep about 6.6 h. After resistance sessions, they took longer to fall asleep (29 vs 10 min) but woke less in the night (31 vs 48 min) than after endurance sessions. **[Research, n = 8]** https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-026-06187-9
- **Fernández-Navarrete et al. 2026, participation.** 278,063 participants in 145 races over 5 seasons, 22 host countries. No performance data. https://doi.org/10.1080/02701367.2025.2584226
- **Fernández-Navarrete et al. 2026, "Mapping the physiological demands of Hyrox©: Which sections hit hardest?"** *Ger J Exerc Sport Res.* https://doi.org/10.1007/s12662-026-01151-3. I could not read the abstract or text. Its findings are not included here.
- **Li F, Xu J, Liu Y 2026, sled push trial.** *Front Sport Res.* 2026;8(3):7–14. https://doi.org/10.25236/FSR.2026.080302. 30 trained men, 8 weeks, 2 sessions a week. Sled-specific training cut 50 m sled push time by 3.86 s. Squat-based training cut it by 1.59 s. Squat training raised the back-squat 1RM more.
  - **Why to distrust it:** The methods are written in the future tense, as if it were a plan. Both groups report the same standard deviation (1.10 s) before and after. The effect sizes are very large (d = 3.51). There is no trial registration in the text I read. The publisher is not a mainstream sports-science publisher. One author is from a sports technology company. **[Unverified]** despite its "trial" label.
- **Bartsch 2026, 80/20 review.** Found no HYROX trial of 80/20 intensity training. Concludes that a universal best intensity split is unproven. The journal's standing is unclear. https://doi.org/10.66308/air.e2026083

## 4. What race-split data show

### 4.1 How finish time divides

- **[Research]** Running is about 48–51% of total time in the Top 100 PRO results of both sexes, across seven seasons (Rappelt et al. 2026).
- **[Research]** In a slower recreational sample, runs took 51.2 of 86.5 min (Brandt et al. 2025). **[Unverified, my arithmetic]** That is about 59%. The runs were on a treadmill, so this may not match a real venue.
- **[Research]** Roxzone is the slowest "running" part (10.5 km/h in PRO men; Gutiérrez-Hellín et al. 2026). It is one of the three largest single sources of between-athlete differences in male PRO results (Yustres Amores et al. 2026).
- **[Practice]** Roxzone length differs by venue. So split comparisons are only fair within one event. **[Research]** support: Rappelt et al. could not study Roxzone pacing because layouts vary, and Shi et al. found event structure changes the results of split analysis.
- **[Unverified]** A Springer case study is said to cite an official HYROX analysis of the 2025 World Championship Elite 15, with running at 52.0–55.7% (men) and 51.4–54.9% (women). I saw this only in a search snippet. I could not find or read the official analysis.

### 4.2 What separates faster and slower finishers

- **[Research]** Running rank tracks overall rank more closely than any station rank (Rappelt et al. 2026).
- **[Research]** In male PRO results, running plus Roxzone and stations each explain about half of the differences between athletes. Wall balls, sled pull, and Roxzone are the largest single parts (Yustres Amores et al. 2026).
- **[Research]** Strength stations cost slower athletes far more time per unit than they cost faster athletes (Rappelt et al. 2026). The news summary of Olivares-Llorente et al. reports the same pattern. **[Unverified]** for that summary.
- **[Research]** Among top athletes, a "strong runner" profile and a "strong station" profile reach similar total times (Yustres Amores et al. 2026). So there is more than one route to a fast time.
- **[Research]** Lab correlates of finish time: VO2max, endurance training volume, and low body fat. Grip strength and resistance-training volume showed no clear link (Brandt et al. 2025, n = 11). Lower-body strength was not measured.
- **[Research]** In PRO men, a higher run speed relative to one's own VT2 seems central to a faster run total (Gutiérrez-Hellín et al. 2026, n = 24).
- **[Practice]** Many coaches say "running is 50% or more of the race, so train running first." This is consistent with the data above. But the data also show that stations explain about half of the differences between male PRO athletes, and much more time loss in slower athletes. So "running first" is not a rule for every athlete.

### 4.3 How run pace changes across the race ("compromised running")

"Compromised running" is a coaching term. It means running while tired from a station. It is not a defined scientific measure.

- **[Research]** Run speed falls across the race. In 24 PRO men it fell from 16.1 km/h (run 1) to 13.6 km/h (run 8) (Gutiérrez-Hellín et al. 2026).
- **[Research]** Run 8 shows the clearest slowing (Li et al. 2026). In the top 50 PRO men, late-race slowing persisted across seven seasons, even as profiles became more even (Gutiérrez-Hellín et al. 2026, *Appl Sci*).
- **[Research]** In a recreational simulation, run 5 (after burpee broad jumps) was the slowest, then runs 6 and 8 (Brandt et al. 2025, n = 11, treadmill).
- **[Unverified, preprint]** The run after sandbag lunges (run 8) and the run after the sled push (run 3) slow the most. The run after the row (run 6) slows least (Zhu et al. 2026).
- **[Research]** In top-ranked results, neither split variability nor late fade was clearly linked to finish time once event and repeat athletes were handled (Shi et al. 2026).
- **[Research, n = 1]** In one world champion, step rate during runs seemed to change after certain stations (Schwalm et al. 2026).
- **[Unverified, my reading]** Together, these suggest that some slowing is normal and expected. A large late fade is not, by itself, proof of poor fitness or poor pacing.

### 4.4 Sex, age, and doubles

- **[Research]** Both sexes got much faster over seven seasons. Top 100 times fell about 19% in men and about 21% in women. Running stayed about half of the race in both (Rappelt et al. 2026).
- **[Unverified, news summary]** The shape of run pacing barely differs by sex. Sex differences sit mainly in the stations (Olivares-Llorente et al. 2026). Loads differ by sex in PRO, so this is not a pure sex effect.
- **[Research]** Benchmark tables exist for individual, doubles, and relay categories (Fernández-Navarrete et al. 2026). I could not see whether they split by age group.
- **Not found:** No peer-reviewed study of age-group effects in HYROX. No physiological study of doubles or relay racing. The only doubles figure is one athlete's best time (Schwalm et al. 2026).
- **[Unverified, blog]** HYROX Data Lab claims that the sex gap is 17.7% at age 16–24 and 9.8% at age 30–34, using 4,309 men and 943 women from three events. The site would not load for me, and the method is not peer reviewed.

### 4.5 Non-peer-reviewed split analyses

- **[Unverified]** Many websites analyse HYROX splits (for example Rox Lyfe, HYROX Data Lab, rox-coach, hyranking). Most do not state their data, sample, or method. One Rox Lyfe article I checked gave pace targets with no data source.
- **[Practice]** These tools can help an athlete compare their own splits with others in the same event. They are not evidence for training methods.

## 6. Injury and health

- **[Research]** Chittenden et al. 2026, *Front Sports Act Living*, https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2026.1937574. 89 athletes (56 women) reported weekly for 12 weeks. Response rate was 62%.
  - Weekly prevalence of any health problem: 30%. Substantial problems: 18%.
  - Injury rate: 14.4 per 1,000 hours. Time-loss injuries: 6.4 per 1,000 hours.
  - Two thirds of injuries were blamed on HYROX training (9.6 per 1,000 h).
  - 73% were overuse injuries. The knee was most common (22%). Knee and lower leg carried the most burden.
  - Previous injury had the largest rate ratio (2.60), but the interval (1.00–6.75) is also compatible with no effect.
  - Only half of problems caused missed training. Counting missed days alone understates the problem.
- **[Unverified, preprint]** Ketzer et al. 2026, medRxiv, https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.08.09.26359590. 418 athletes, online survey.
  - 49.8% reported at least one HYROX-related injury in 12 months.
  - Most were gradual-onset, lower-limb, and tendon-related. The knee was most common (20.8%).
  - 20.3% reported more than 28 days off or no return to their previous level.
  - More HYROX-specific training sessions per week went with more injury reports (adjusted odds ratio 1.61). The design cannot show cause.
- **[Research, older and broader]** Williams et al. 2020 recorded 26 medical-attention injuries in 1,085 competitors at two "functional fitness racing" events: 36 per 1,000 competition hours. The abstract does not name the event brand, so it may not be HYROX. https://doi.org/10.70252/cdzf5022
- **[Practice]** Because overuse knee and lower-leg problems dominate, coaches watch total running load and lunge and sled volume. No HYROX study tests a prevention program.

## 7. What the evidence does not establish

- **No validated training split.** No trial compares different ratios of running, strength, and station work for HYROX. The 80/20 idea is untested in HYROX (Bartsch 2026).
- **No good-quality training trial.** The only HYROX "trial" found (sled push) has serious reporting problems.
- **Small lab samples.** The only full-race lab study had 11 people. The only race-day physiology study had 24 men. One case study had 1 athlete.
- **Few women in physiology studies.** Brandt had 3 women. HYScience had none. The case study was a man.
- **No race-day heart rate or lactate in a real event.** Brandt's data come from a treadmill simulation with a non-standard sled.
- **Correlation is not cause.** A higher VO2max goes with faster times, but no study shows that raising VO2max by a given amount lowers HYROX time by a given amount.
- **Split data are messy.** Venue layouts differ. Some results are self-reported. Repeat athletes change the results. Equipment and rules changed across seasons.
- **No age-group or doubles physiology.** Benchmarks exist, but nothing explains how age or partner racing changes demands.
- **No pacing trial.** No study assigns athletes to different pacing plans in HYROX. Advice on run 1 pace comes from observation and other sports.
- **No taper, nutrition, or heat study in HYROX.** All advice there comes from other sports.
- **No injury-prevention trial.** Injury data are descriptive.
- **No validated test battery.** No study shows which lab or field tests best predict an individual's HYROX time.

## 8. Using this in coaching: research, practice, and individual hypotheses

This section shows how a coaching skill can keep the three levels apart. Each line uses the same labels.

| Topic | What research shows | Common coaching practice | Hypothesis to test in one athlete |
|---|---|---|---|
| Training focus | **[Research]** Running is about half the race. Strength stations cost slower athletes more. | **[Practice]** Build aerobic running first. Fix the station that costs the most time. | "This athlete loses most time at wall balls and sled pull. Two station sessions a week for 8 weeks will cut those splits without slowing their 5 km." Test with a repeat race or simulation. |
| Concurrent training | See Section 5.1. | **[Practice]** Separate hard runs and heavy leg work by several hours, or do them on different days. | "Heavy lower-body days on hard-run days are why this athlete's threshold runs feel flat." Test by moving them and comparing run quality. |
| Run pacing | **[Research]** Speed falls across the race. Fade size is not clearly linked to finish time in top athletes. | **[Practice]** Run 1 a little slower than goal pace. Hold steady through the middle. | "This athlete's run 1 is too fast, because runs 6–8 fall more than 10% behind runs 2–3." Test with a planned run 1 pace in the next race. |
| Station-to-run transition | **[Unverified, preprint]** Runs after lunges and sled push slow most. | **[Practice]** Brick sessions: station then run. | "This athlete's run after lunges is their worst transition." Check their own splits first. |
| Taper | See Section 5.5. | **[Practice]** Cut volume, keep some intensity, for 1–2 weeks. | "A 10-day taper with 40–50% less volume leaves this athlete fresher than a 5-day taper." Compare how they feel and perform. |
| Injury | **[Research]** Overuse knee and lower-leg problems are common. | **[Practice]** Increase running load gradually. Rotate lunge and sled volume. | "This athlete's knee pain follows weeks with high lunge volume." Track load and symptoms. |

Rules for the skill:

1. State the evidence level of every claim it makes to an athlete.
2. Never present a coaching practice as a research finding.
3. Treat each athlete's data as the test of any hypothesis. One race or one session is weak evidence.
4. Compare splits only within the same event, because venue layouts differ.
5. Say plainly when no HYROX evidence exists.
