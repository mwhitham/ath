## How to read this file

Every claim carries one label:

- **[Official]**: stated by CrossFit, LLC. Sources are the Level 1 Training Guide, crossfit.com, or games.crossfit.com. Official guidance is CrossFit's own position. It is not proof that the method works.
- **[Research]**: stated in a peer-reviewed paper. The study's limits follow the finding.
- **[Practice]**: common coaching practice, or a reading of the sources by the author of this file. Neither CrossFit nor a study has confirmed it. Treat it as a reasonable default, not a fact.

**[Unconfirmed]** marks anything that could not be checked against a primary source.

Citation forms:

- **L1 p. N** is the *CrossFit Level 1 Training Guide*, Third Edition, © 2020 CrossFit, LLC, document code V6E3OL-20201212KW. N is the printed page number ("N of 255"). In the PDF file, the page index is the printed number plus 2. For example, printed p. 77 is PDF page 79.
- **RB26 §x** is the 2026 CrossFit Games Rulebook at https://games.crossfit.com/rules. It was published 2025-12-10 and last modified 2025-12-11.
- Web pages are cited by URL. All were read on 2026-11-15.
- Studies are cited by first author and year. DOIs are given in section 4 and in the reference list.

Short terms used below:

- **Rx** (or Rx'd): the workout "as prescribed".
- **AMRAP**: as many rounds and reps as possible in a fixed time.
- **RPE**: rating of perceived exertion, a number for how hard a session felt.
- **sRPE**: session RPE. It is the RPE multiplied by session minutes.
- **HIFT**: high-intensity functional training. Research papers use this name for CrossFit-style training.

## 1. CrossFit Level 1 Training Guide

### 1.1 Fitness, intensity, and health

- [Official] CrossFit's prescription is "constantly varied, high-intensity functional movement." (L1 p. 2)
- [Official] Functional movements are described as "universal motor recruitment patterns." They are multi-joint movements, and they move "from core to extremity." (L1 p. 2)
- [Official] "Intensity is defined exactly as power." (L1 p. 2) Power is "force times distance (work) divided by time." (L1 p. 36)
- [Official] CrossFit says its program "increases work capacity across broad time and modal domains." (L1 p. 3)
- [Official] Fitness is defined from a person's set of workout results: "your collection of workout data points represented your work capacity across broad time and modal domains. This is your fitness." (L1 p. 36)
- [Official] Fitness can be drawn as the area under a curve. The curve plots power against the length of the effort. Health is that fitness held across age. (L1 pp. 36–38)
- [Official] CrossFit uses four models of fitness (L1 pp. 18–21, 33–34):
  - ten general physical skills;
  - performance on random tasks (the "hopper");
  - three energy pathways;
  - a sickness–wellness–fitness scale of health markers.
- [Official] The ten general physical skills are (L1 p. 18):
  1. cardiovascular/respiratory endurance
  2. stamina
  3. strength
  4. flexibility
  5. power
  6. speed
  7. coordination
  8. agility
  9. balance
  10. accuracy

  Endurance, stamina, strength, and flexibility improve through "training." Coordination, agility, balance, and accuracy improve through "practice," meaning changes in the nervous system. Power and speed come from both. (L1 p. 18)
- [Official] The three energy pathways (L1 p. 34):
  - Phosphagen: "about 100 percent of max human output," and "taps out at about 10 seconds."
  - Glycolytic (lactate): about 70 percent of max power. It "peaks at about 60 seconds and terminates at 120 seconds."
  - Oxidative (aerobic): about 40 percent of max power, for long efforts.
- [Official] The "theoretical hierarchy" of athlete development, from base to top (L1 p. 29):
  1. nutrition
  2. metabolic conditioning
  3. gymnastics
  4. weightlifting
  5. sport

  The guide says this model "has greatest utility in analyzing athletes' shortcomings."
- [Official] "The needs of an Olympic athlete and our grandparents differ by degree not kind." "We scale load and intensity; we do not change programs." (L1 p. 31)
- [Official] "Routine is the enemy of progress and broad adaptation." (L1 p. 30)
- [Official] On the hopper model: the larger gain comes from working on "that event or skill that you do not want to see come out of the hopper." (L1 p. 33)
- [Official] The guide also says that technique and intensity are linked: "In CrossFit, if your technique is perfect, your intensity is always low." The coach raises speed until form starts to fail. The athlete then fixes form at that speed. The guide calls this "threshold training." (L1 pp. 43–44)

### 1.2 "Mechanics, consistency, then intensity"

- [Official] "CrossFit's charter for creating the most optimal balance of safety, efficacy, and efficiency is: mechanics, consistency, then—and only then—intensity." (L1 p. 77)
- [Official] Movements must be done "correctly and consistently before load and speed are added." The guide says that ignoring this order "increases the risk for injury and potentially blunts long-term progress." (L1 p. 77)
- [Official] The same order is listed as the first defense against rhabdomyolysis. Rhabdomyolysis is muscle breakdown that can damage the kidneys. (L1 p. 151)
- [Official] The guide states the order in short form: "'Mechanics,' then 'Consistency,' and then 'Intensity'— this is the key to effective implementation of CrossFit programming." (L1 p. 40, sidebar quote)

### 1.3 Scaling and "preserve the stimulus"

**Core rule**

- [Official] "When scaling workouts, the main principle to follow is 'preserve the stimulus.'" (L1 p. 77)
- [Official] "The stimulus of the workout refers to the effects of the specific combination of movements, time domain, and load." The parts can be adjusted so the workout gives "relatively similar effects on each athlete." (L1 p. 77)
- [Official] Scaling means to "adhere to as many of the original workout factors as possible relative to the individual's physical and psychological tolerances." (L1 p. 83)
- [Official] No single rule covers every case. The athlete's judgment, or a qualified trainer's, decides. (L1 p. 77)

**Order of what to change**

- [Official] Two factors must be scaled for every beginner: intensity and volume. A "prudent method" is to cut intensity and/or volume "by half for at least two weeks." (L1 p. 78)
- [Official] Intensity can be changed through load, speed, or volume. (L1 p. 78)
- [Official] Load: "Load is the variable to scale first." (L1 p. 78)
  - In conditioning workouts, choose a load that lets the athlete finish the first set or round with good form and without muscular failure. (L1 p. 78)
  - New athletes often use "less than 50 percent of the prescribed load." (L1 p. 78)
  - "Coaches should err on the side of scaling too much rather than not enough." (L1 p. 78)
- [Official] Speed is mostly self-limited. A coach may slow an athlete down to fix mechanics, or ask an athlete who moves well to go faster. (L1 p. 78)
- [Official] Volume can be lowered by reducing "1) time; 2) reps/rounds; and/or 3) distance." (L1 p. 78)
- [Official] Volume is reduced for beginners because "muscles, ligaments, and tendons need to become gradually accustomed to the volume." This also reduces soreness and rhabdomyolysis risk. (L1 p. 78)
- [Official] Movement: change the movement last. Avoiding a movement keeps the athlete from getting better at it. Reduce the load first. For example, snatch with a PVC pipe rather than swapping in 95-lb overhead squats. (L1 p. 79)
- [Official] Swap a movement only for a physical limit, an injury, or when the load cannot be reduced. Match the original on four points (L1 p. 79):
  1. lower body or upper body
  2. function, such as push or pull
  3. range of motion, especially at the hips, knees, and ankles
  4. plane of movement

**Time domain**

- [Official] Scaled times "should be relatively similar" to advanced athletes' times. Example: an advanced athlete may finish Fran in 2 minutes, but a new athlete doing it as prescribed may take 15 minutes or more. The guide says "Fran should be completed within several minutes." (L1 p. 78)
- [Official] The Fran lesson plan gives these targets (L1 pp. 88, 91):
  - Finish "in less than 10 minutes."
  - A scaled athlete who finished last time "under 5 minutes" should get a harder version.
  - The suggested split is no more than three breaks in the 21s, two in the 15s, and one in the 9s.
- [Official] crossfit.com gives a different target. It calls finishing Fran in 10–12 minutes "a great goal for all athletes." It suggests a thruster load that allows "at least 15 unbroken reps." (https://www.crossfit.com/benchmark/fran) The two Fran time targets differ slightly. **[Practice]** Treat about 10 minutes as the upper limit for a scaled Fran.
- [Official] Cindy scaled example: the 20-minute AMRAP becomes a 10-minute AMRAP, or 10 rounds for time. Reps per round can also drop so the athlete keeps moving instead of reaching failure early. (L1 p. 80)

**During the workout**

- [Official] "At the appearance of unsafe form, the athlete or coach should end the workout or reduce the load to that which allows proper mechanics." (L1 p. 77)
- [Official] "Progressive scaling" must be avoided with beginner or intermediate athletes. Progressive scaling means lowering the load again and again so an exhausted athlete never has to stop. Let them rest instead. (L1 p. 152)
- [Official] A later crossfit.com article allows the opposite change. If the scaled version is easier than expected, "the repetitions, loading, or distance may be increased slightly in the later rounds." Ending the workout early is acceptable when movement quality declines. (https://www.crossfit.com/essentials/professional-training-scaling-part-2, 2020-01-19)

**How long to scale**

- [Official] "A month is the minimum period for which significant scaling should be applied." Scaling of load "might continue for months and years." (L1 pp. 77, 82)
- [Official] "Multiple months at scaled loads and volumes are well within a normal timeframe for even the best athletes." (L1 p. 152)
- [Official] Athletes at higher rhabdomyolysis risk (L1 p. 153):
  - fit people from other training who are new to CrossFit;
  - people returning after a layoff;
  - experienced athletes who go "significantly outside their established 'norm.'"
- [Official] Keep "negatives" (slow lowering phases) low for beginners. Jumping pull-ups and full-range GHD sit-ups are named as the usual sources. (L1 p. 152)

**Newer crossfit.com guidance (2020)**

Source: https://www.crossfit.com/essentials/professional-training-scaling-part-2 (author "CrossFit", 2020-01-19).

- [Official] "Scaling should be used to meet the athlete at their current level, preserving the originally intended stimulus of the workout."
- [Official] "Scaling should be viewed as a progressive tool to move athletes beyond their current level, not a crutch used to avoid difficulty."
- [Official] First consider the factors that set intensity: "loads, distances (reps), and time (duration of the effort)."
- [Official] If a movement is still too hard, break it into a movement progression. Example: squat snatches become snatch-grip deadlifts, hang power snatches, and overhead squats with a PVC pipe or empty bar.
- [Official] Swapping or omitting a movement should be "reserved as a final step." When it is needed, "the loading, level of challenge, and function of each movement, should be preserved."
- [Official] Vary scaling choices from workout to workout. This keeps athletes from "avoiding elements that challenge them most."

### 1.4 The theoretical programming template

- [Official] "In the broadest view we see a three-days-on, one-day-off pattern." The guide says this "allows for a relatively higher volume of high-intensity work." (L1 p. 71)
- [Official] By the fourth day, "neuromuscular function and anatomy are hammered." (L1 p. 72)
- [Official] A five-days-on, two-days-off pattern is offered for people on a work week: "If life is easier with the five-on, two-off pattern, do not hesitate to employ it." (L1 p. 72)
- [Official] Status of the template: "CrossFit.com has never used this template for its programming." Its purpose is "as much descriptive as prescriptive." (L1 p. 71)

**Modalities (L1 p. 73)**

| Code | Modality | Purpose given in the guide | Exercises listed |
|---|---|---|---|
| M | Monostructural metabolic conditioning ("cardio") | cardiorespiratory capacity and stamina | run, bike, row, jump rope |
| G | Gymnastics (body weight) | body control, coordination, balance, agility, accuracy, upper-body and trunk strength | air squat, pull-up, push-up, dip, handstand push-up, rope climb, muscle-up, press to handstand, back extension, sit-up, jump, lunge |
| W | Weightlifting (any external load) | strength, power, hip and leg capacity | deadlift, cleans, press, snatch, clean and jerk, medicine-ball drills, kettlebell swing |

**Workout structure (L1 pp. 74–76, Table 3)**

| Element count | Days in 12-day cycle | Priority | Structure | Intensity | Work and rest |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| One (single) | 1, 5, 9 | Element priority | M: one long effort. G: practice of one skill. W: one heavy lift | M: long, slow distance. G: high skill. W: heavy | Recovery is not a limiting factor |
| Two (couplet) | 2, 6, 10 | Task priority: the work is fixed and time varies | Couplet repeated 3–5 times for time | Two moderately to intensely challenging elements | Managing work and rest is critical |
| Three (triplet) | 3, 7, 11 | Time priority: the time is fixed and the work varies | Triplet repeated for 20 minutes, scored by rounds | Three lightly to moderately challenging elements | Managing work and rest is a minor factor |

- [Official] For a couplet, "the first round is hard but possible." Later rounds need pacing and rest. (L1 p. 75)
- [Official] As the number of elements rises, the effect comes "less to the individual element selected and more to the effect of repeated efforts." (L1 p. 76)
- [Official] The single "G" day practices a skill that "might not be yet suitable for inclusion in a timed workout." (L1 p. 74)
- [Official] Another pattern the guide likes (L1 pp. 30–31). It adds: "There is nothing sacred in this pattern."
  1. warm up;
  2. "3 to 5 sets of 3 to 5 reps of a fundamental lift";
  3. a 10-minute gymnastics circuit "at a blistering pace";
  4. 2–10 minutes of high-intensity conditioning.
- [Official] "A strength and conditioning regimen devoid of gymnastics practice and skills is deficient." (L1 p. 74, sidebar quote)

**Newer crossfit.com guidance (2023)**

Source: https://www.crossfit.com/pro-coach/programming-basics-part-2 (Eric O'Connor, CF-L4, 2023-11-20).

- [Official] Weekly goals for a generalist: "one heavy-focused session, one short workout, one long workout, and three classic workouts." Classic workouts are "sub-15 minutes, couplets, triplets, complementary movement pairings, and high power."
- [Official] Sample time domains:
  - short: under 5 minutes;
  - couplet: 7–10 minutes;
  - triplet: 8–15 or 10–15 minutes;
  - long: 20+ minutes.
- [Official] "Two-part workouts are not prioritized due to affecting intensity negatively on one or both sections."
- [Official] Plan variance instead of random choice. Review one to two weeks of programming and look for gaps. Common gaps are moderate loads, longer workouts, monostructural work, and higher-skill elements. "Our goal is not to solve for every deficiency in the next two workouts."
- [Official] Targeted blocks last "six to eight weeks." Bookend them with testing. The example is a 3-rep-max back squat test, then back-squat work twice per week.
- [Official] Judge the program by "repeating benchmarks and comparing the results to previous scores... The data doesn't lie."
- [Official] Programming "is just one piece of an effective program." It is likely secondary to technique, coaching, environment, nutrition, and the ability to work at high intensity.

### 1.5 Extra work around class

- [Official] crossfit.com defines accessory work as "exercises uncommon in standard workouts performed before or after the main training session." Its guidance (https://www.crossfit.com/pro-coach/accessory-work-in-crossfit-part-1, Eric O'Connor, CF-L4, 2025-03-19):
  - "Accessory exercises have their place but are rarely essential for most CrossFit athletes."
  - Accessory work is "best used as part of the post-workout period."
  - "Focus on one well-executed accessory movement per session."
  - Watch weekly balance. Hitting the same body region on back-to-back days raises overuse risk.
  - "Accessory work is a complement, not a replacement, for the foundational principles of CrossFit."
- [Official] Valid reasons the same article gives for accessory work: rehabilitation, imbalances, enjoyment, and weak points in experienced athletes. One example is triceps work for handstand push-up lockout.
- [Official] Extra volume costs intensity: "if you increase the volume, your intensity will decrease in some of the pieces you're doing." The advice is "Go as hard as good technique will allow in that one workout." (https://www.crossfit.com/essentials/does-more-workout-volume-mean-more-fitness, Luis Fernandez, CF-L3, 2023-05-18)
- [Practice] Before adding extra work, check three things. Does it repeat a pattern the class already loaded this week? Does it come before the class workout and blunt it? Can the athlete still meet class intensity the next day?

### 1.6 The nine foundational movements

- [Official] The nine foundational movements of the Level 1 Course (L1 p. 170):
  1. air squat
  2. front squat
  3. overhead squat
  4. shoulder press
  5. push press
  6. push jerk
  7. deadlift
  8. sumo deadlift high pull
  9. medicine-ball clean
- [Official] The four additional movements taught at the course are the pull-up, thruster, muscle-up, and snatch. (L1 p. 218)
- [Official] The movement pages say each movement "builds on" or "carries over from" an earlier one. This gives three families:
  - air squat → front squat → overhead squat (L1 pp. 171, 176, 178);
  - shoulder press → push press → push jerk (L1 pp. 184, 191);
  - deadlift → sumo deadlift high pull → medicine-ball clean (L1 pp. 201, 208).
- [Official] "The kipping pull-up is CrossFit's default pull-up." "CrossFit recommends athletes develop a strict pull-up before performing kipping pull-ups." (L1 p. 219)
- [Official] "CrossFit recommends athletes achieve a strict muscle-up before attempting kipping muscle-ups." (L1 p. 232)
- [Official] "Teaching a snatch where there is not yet an overhead squat, teaching an overhead squat where there is not yet an air squat, is a colossal mistake." (L1 p. 159)

### 1.7 Common faults coaches look for

[Official] Each movement page lists faults and corrections. The faults below are taken directly from the guide.

| Movement | Faults named in the guide | L1 pages |
|---|---|---|
| Air squat | Loss of neutral spine (lumbar flexion). Weight on or shifting to toes. Not going low enough. Hips do not travel back and knees move too far forward. Knees not tracking in line with toes. "Multiple-fault squat." "Immature squat" (all points met, but the athlete leans far forward onto the quads) | 172–175 |
| Front squat | All air squat faults, plus: bar not in contact with the torso in the rack. Elbows drop during the squat | 177 |
| Overhead squat | All air squat faults, plus: inactive overhead position (bent elbows or inactive shoulders). Bar moves forward of the frontal plane | 179 |
| Shoulder press | Overextending the spine with ribs sticking out. Bar finishes forward of the frontal plane. Elbows bent or shoulders not active. Bar arcs around the face | 181–183 |
| Push press | Chest inclines forward in the dip. "Muted hips" (hips push forward in the dip). Pressing early, before the hips extend | 186–187 |
| Push jerk | All press and push press faults, plus: lack of full hip extension. Poor overhead position when receiving. Lowering the bar before standing all the way up | 191–193 |
| Deadlift | Loss of lumbar curve (the correction is to abort and lower the load). Weight on toes. Shoulders behind the bar, or hips too high, in the set-up. Hips do not move back to start the descent. Bar loses contact with legs. Hips rise before the chest. Shoulders rise without the hips, so the bar goes around the knees | 195–200 |
| Sumo deadlift high pull | All deadlift faults, plus: pulling early (shrug or arm bend before full hip extension). Elbows low and inside. Hips flex before arms extend on the descent. Shoulders roll forward | 204–207 |
| Medicine-ball clean | Lack of full hip extension. Curling the ball. Collapsing in the receiving position. Receiving too high. Tossing the ball without pulling under. Not standing up before lowering | 212–217 |
| Pull-up | Starting the swing with the legs. Not pushing away after clearing the bar. Losing the midline (overextending or exaggerating the swing) | 224–226 |
| Thruster | Pressing before the hips extend. Descending into the squat before the bar reaches the rack | 230–231 |
| Muscle-up | Losing the false grip. Body too upright in the pull. Elbows flaring. Not pulling the rings low enough before the transition | 236–239 |
| Snatch | Lack of hip extension. Elbows not high and outside, or bar travels around the body. Shoulders rise without the hips. Hips rise without the shoulders | 244–247 |

- [Official] When several faults appear at once, triage them. Fix them "one at a time in order of importance." The guide adds that "no single ordering of faults can be used across all athletes and movements." (L1 p. 144)
- [Official] Good cues are "short, specific and actionable," such as "push the hips back." Any cue that improves the movement is a good cue. (L1 p. 144)

### 1.8 Coaching, class structure, safety, and scope

- [Official] The six trainer skills are teaching, seeing, correcting, group management, presence and attitude, and demonstration. (L1 p. 142)
- [Official] Newer coaches find it hardest to see faults "while athletes are moving." (L1 p. 143)
- [Official] Plan every class with a warm-up, workout, and cool-down. (L1 p. 87)
  - The warm-up should let the coach "assess capacity for scaling modifications."
  - The workout should state range-of-motion standards and offer scaling for every athlete.
  - The cool-down should let the athlete "record workout performance to track progress."
- [Official] "Trainers need to frequently check in with athletes to determine how the previous dose of exercise affected them." (L1 p. 152)
- [Official] Rhabdomyolysis warning signs: "severe generalized muscle pain, nausea and vomiting, abdominal cramping," and "dark-red or cola-colored urine." If these appear, "seek medical attention immediately." (L1 p. 153)
- [Official] A trainer's scope "does not extend to diagnosing or treating any medical condition." Athletes with a medical condition need a physician's clearance. (L1 p. 156)
- [Official] Hydration: "Drink when you are thirsty, do not when you are not." (L1 p. 155)

### 2.2 Notes and differences between sources

- [Official] Grace: "The power clean and jerk variation is the intended variation of the clean and jerk to be performed." (https://www.crossfit.com/benchmark/grace)

The Rx box and the 2004 Journal both give: deadlift 1½ × body weight, bench press 1 × body weight, clean ¾ × body weight. (https://www.crossfit.com/benchmark/linda; CFJ Issue 27)

### 2.3 How CrossFit says to compare repeat attempts

- [Official] The work in a benchmark stays constant only while load, range of motion, height, and body weight stay the same. Then "the difference in time is the difference in power produced." (L1 p. 36)
- [Official] Example: a Fran time that drops from 4:30 to 2:45 equals about a 60% rise in power. (L1 p. 35)
- [Official] Progress means "faster with the same weight or at a similar time with increased weight." (https://www.crossfit.com/benchmark/fran)
- [Practice] Compare only like with like: the same version, load, movement standard, and pull-up style (strict, kipping, or butterfly). If any of these changed, record it next to the score and do not treat the two times as directly comparable.

### 3.3 Rx, Scaled, and Foundations

- [Official] Each Rx workout "may be accompanied by scaled or foundations versions." Athletes may mix versions across the Open. (RB26 §1.29)
- [Official] Ranking per workout (RB26 §1.29):
  - Scaled athletes are ranked among Scaled athletes, "but below all athletes who performed that workout as prescribed."
  - Foundations athletes are ranked "below all athletes who performed that workout as scaled and as prescribed."
- [Official] A missing score counts as "0." It ranks below every athlete who entered a score in any version. Such an athlete gets no "level." (RB26 §§1.26, 1.29)
- [Official] There is a scaled leaderboard (only Scaled or Foundations scores) and a Foundations-only leaderboard. (RB26 §1.29)
- [Official] Foundations loads can be suggestions. The 26.3 Foundations scorecard says: "The loads are a suggested starting point. If you are completing all the workouts in the Foundations division, you are free to decrease or increase the load as your skill level allows." (https://games-assets.crossfit.com/s3fs-public/2026-03/CFG26%20Open%2026.3%20Scorecard_Foundations_V6_wiwgn.pdf)
- [Official] An athlete who cannot reach an Rx range of motion may not substitute a movement or use outside aid. They "should consider submitting the score in the scaled or foundations tier." (RB26 §1.19)
- [Official] "Only CrossFit may provide official scaled versions of workouts." Modifying the format "will void an athlete's score." (RB26 §1.17)

### 3.4 How a capped "for time" result is scored

- [Official] The 2026 Open scorecards rank capped athletes by reps, with a tiebreak time. 26.1 (12-minute cap) says: "In the event of a tie on reps, the athlete with the faster tiebreak will win the tie. Athletes who complete the workout under the time cap will not enter a tiebreak score." The 26.3 Foundations scorecard (16-minute cap) uses the same wording. (26.1 and 26.3 scorecard URLs above)
- [Official] The team rule states the order outright. A time-capped team's "score will be its total reps completed." The team "will be ranked below any team whose score is a valid time and above any team that completes fewer reps." (RB26 §1.31)
- [Official] A penalty can push a time past the cap. The score then becomes the reps done inside the cap. Example: Fran (90 reps, 6-minute cap) submitted at 5:30 with a 60-second penalty. The athlete's last 30 seconds of work is removed. The score "will be displayed as a time-capped score," here 81 of 90 reps. (RB26 §4.11; also Appendix E)
- [Practice] So, for individuals: any finisher ranks above any capped athlete. Capped athletes rank by reps, then by tiebreak time. The 2026 rulebook has no single sentence that states this for individual Open athletes. It follows from the scorecards and the team rule.
- [Practice] No 2026 rule was found that turns a capped score into a time, such as cap plus one second per missing rep. Do not invent an "estimated finish time" for capped results when comparing Open scores.

### 3.5 Tiebreaks and overall ranking

- [Official] Each workout sets its own tiebreak point. 26.1: "A tiebreak time will be taken after each set of box jump-overs and each set of box step-overs. The LAST completed set... will be your tiebreak time." 26.3: after each set of thrusters. (26.1 and 26.3 scorecards)
- [Official] The overall Open rank is the sum of workout placings. The lower total ranks higher. Example: placings 2 + 3 + 5 = 10 beat placings 1 + 2 + 10 = 13. (RB26 §1.25)
- [Official] Overall ties go to the athlete with the best single-workout placing, then the next best, and so on. (RB26 §1.25)
- [Official] "Ties will not be broken for single workout results." Athletes with identical results share the rank and points. (RB26 §1.25)
- [Official] Ranking for advancement uses the worldwide leaderboard. Region leaderboards are "simply a leaderboard filter." (RB26 §§1.27–1.28)
- [Official] The 2027 Open page says Quarterfinals take "the top 25% of individual and age-group athletes from the Open." (https://games.crossfit.com/open/overview)

### 3.6 Judging, validation, and video standards

- [Official] An Open score is validated in one of two ways (RB26 §1.20):
  - a judge at a licensed affiliate in good standing; or
  - a public YouTube video.
- [Official] Judges at affiliates need no credential: "There is no credentialing requirement for judging at a CrossFit affiliate during the Open." Online review judges must hold the current Judges Course or the Advanced Judges Course certificate. (RB26 §1.18)
- [Official] Affiliate scores need no video. They are validated automatically unless the affiliate manager rejects them by Wednesday 5 p.m. PT. (RB26 §§1.21, 1.26)
- [Official] Video outcomes after review are "Good Video," "Valid With Penalty," "Zero (0) Score," and "Invalid." Invalid removes all of the athlete's scores for the competition. (RB26 §1.23)
- [Official] A score may be modified for any of these reasons (RB26 §1.22):
  - bad angles or lighting;
  - fisheye lenses;
  - a blocked view of the athlete;
  - standard violations;
  - clothing that hides the standards;
  - miscounted reps.
- [Official] Video requirements whose breach "may result in a 0 score" (RB26 Appendix D):
  - show weights are correct;
  - show setup measurements;
  - follow the rep scheme;
  - submit unedited video;
  - use any camera placement the floor plan requires.
- [Official] Video best practices (RB26 Appendix D):
  - a "¾ view" of the athlete;
  - the camera at least 3 ft (90 cm) off the ground;
  - "a clock or timer that counts up from 0" visible throughout;
  - no fisheye lens.
- [Official] Barbells: men use a 45-lb bar and women a 35-lb bar. Official conversions include 95 lb = 43 kg, 135 lb = 61 kg, 225 lb = 102 kg, 53-lb kettlebell = 24 kg, and 20-lb ball = 9 kg. (RB26 Appendix D)

### 3.7 Penalties (Online Semifinals rules)

- [Official] No-reps add scripted time per rep. The example is 5 no-reps × 5 seconds = 25 seconds. (RB26 §4.11)
- [Official] A false start costs 10 seconds. Work that cannot be seen at all earns credit only up to the unverifiable reps. (RB26 §4.11)
- [Official] An incorrect setup (load, measurements, camera) "may receive a 0 score." (RB26 §4.11)
- [Official] Open video penalties "may vary by movement and context." (RB26 §1.23)

### 3.8 What this means when comparing scores

- [Practice] Compare Rx with Rx, Scaled with Scaled, and Foundations with Foundations. The rulebook ranks the versions as separate tiers, not on one scale. (RB26 §1.29)
- [Practice] Do not compare Foundations scores between athletes unless the loads are known. Foundations loads may be changed. (26.3 Foundations scorecard)
- [Practice] Compare age-group scores against the same Rx loads. 16–17 and 35–54 match the individual Rx. 14–15 and 55+ may not. (RB26 §1.30; 26.1 scorecard)
- [Practice] A finished time always beats a capped score. Two capped scores compare by reps first, then by tiebreak time.
- [Practice] Affiliate-judged scores are checked less strictly than reviewed video scores. Affiliate judges need no credential, and affiliate scores need no video. (RB26 §§1.18, 1.21) Treat a single score as having some error.
- [Practice] Report Open results as a percentile within the division and version, not only as a raw rank. Field size changes each year.

## 4. Peer-reviewed evidence

Each study gives the citation, link, design, sample, main finding, and limits. All findings are **[Research]**.

### 4.1 Injury rates in CrossFit

**Klimek et al. 2018**
- Citation: Klimek C, Ashbeck C, Brook AJ, Durall C. Are injuries more common with CrossFit training than other forms of exercise? *J Sport Rehabil.* 2018;27(3):295–299.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1123/jsr.2016-0040
- Design: a "critically appraised topic." This is a short focused review, not a full systematic review.
- Sample: 3 retrospective cohort studies (level 2b evidence).
- Finding: injury rates in CrossFit were "comparable or lower than" rates in Olympic weightlifting, distance running, track and field, rugby, and gymnastics. Shoulder injuries "appear to be somewhat common." Injury was more common when supervision was not always available.
- Limits: only 3 studies, with no randomization, control group, or uniform training. The authors call the certainty "questionable."

**Feito et al. 2018**
- Citation: Feito Y, Burrows EK, Tabb LP. A 4-year analysis of the incidence of injuries among CrossFit-trained participants. *Orthop J Sports Med.* 2018;6(10):2325967118803100.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1177/2325967118803100
- Design: descriptive epidemiology. An online survey of people who trained between 2013 and 2017.
- Sample: 3,049 participants worldwide (convenience sample).
- Findings:
  - 30.5% reported an injury in the previous 12 months.
  - The most common sites were shoulders (39%), back (36%), knees (15%), elbows (12%), and wrists (11%).
  - The injury rate was 0.27 per 1,000 hours if the maximum assumed weekly hours were used, and 0.74 per 1,000 hours with the minimum.
  - Risk was higher in the first year of training and with fewer than 3 days or 3 workouts per week.
- Limits: training hours were assumed, not measured. Injuries were self-reported and subject to recall bias, and the authors say minor injuries may be forgotten. The sample came from social media.

**Weisenthal et al. 2014**
- Citation: Weisenthal BM, Beck CA, Maloney MD, DeHaven KE, Giordano BD. Injury rate and patterns among CrossFit athletes. *Orthop J Sports Med.* 2014;2(4):2325967114531177.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1177/2325967114531177
- Design: online survey of injuries in the prior 6 months.
- Sample: 386 eligible participants at US gyms.
- Findings:
  - 19.4% (75 of 386) had at least one injury in 6 months.
  - Men were injured more often than women.
  - The shoulder was the most common site in gymnastics movements, and the low back in powerlifting movements.
  - Injury rate was lower with trainer involvement (P = .028).
- Limits: self-selected respondents, self-reported diagnoses, and a short recall window.

**Hak et al. 2013**
- Citation: Hak PT, Hodzovic E, Hickey B. The nature and prevalence of injury during CrossFit training. *J Strength Cond Res.* Published online 2013-11-22.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000000318
- Design: online questionnaire on international CrossFit forums.
- Sample: 132 respondents.
- Findings: 73.5% had been injured during CrossFit training, with 186 injuries in total. 7.0% of injuries needed surgery. The rate was 3.1 per 1,000 hours. No rhabdomyolysis was reported.
- Limits: very small, self-selected forum sample.

**Montalvo et al. 2017**
- Citation: Montalvo AM, Shaefer H, Rodriguez B, Li T, Epnere K, Myer GD. Retrospective injury epidemiology and risk factors for injury in CrossFit. *J Sports Sci Med.* 2017;16(1):53–59. PMCID: PMC5358031.
- Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28344451/
- Design: retrospective survey covering the prior 6 months.
- Sample: 191 athletes at 4 South Florida gyms.
- Findings:
  - Incidence was 2.3 per 1,000 training hours.
  - Competitors were injured more often (40% vs 19%) and trained more hours per week.
  - Injured athletes had more years of participation, more weekly hours and sessions, and greater height and body mass.
- Limits: small, regional, retrospective. Competitors and exposure are tangled together.

**Rodríguez et al. 2022**
- Citation: Rodríguez MÁ, García-Calleja P, Terrados N, Crespo I, Del Valle M, Olmedillas H. Injury in CrossFit®: a systematic review of epidemiology and risk factors. *Phys Sportsmed.* 2022;50(1):3–10.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1080/00913847.2020.1864675
- Design: systematic review of studies up to August 2020.
- Sample: 25 studies covering 12,079 practitioners.
- Findings:
  - Mean injury prevalence was 35.3%.
  - Incidence ranged from 0.2 to 18.9 per 1,000 hours.
  - The most injured areas were the shoulder (26%), spine (24%), and knee (18%).
  - 8.7% of injuries needed surgery.
  - Reported risk factors were older age, male sex, higher body mass index, previous injury, lack of coach supervision, CrossFit experience, and competing.
- Limits: the authors say the "limited quality of the studies prevents us from drawing solid conclusions about injury risk factors."

**Mehrab et al. 2023**
- Citation: Mehrab M, Wagner RK, Vuurberg G, Gouttebarge V, de Vos RJ, Mathijssen NMC. Risk factors for musculoskeletal injury in CrossFit: a systematic review. *Int J Sports Med.* 2023;44(4):247–257.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1055/a-1953-6317
- Design: systematic review limited to prospective cohort studies, with a best-evidence synthesis.
- Sample: 3 cohort studies covering 691 participants, of whom 172 were injured.
- Findings:
  - There was "limited evidence" that switching back and forth between prescribed and scaled loads raises injury risk. This came from one low-bias study, odds ratio 3.5 (95% CI 1.7–7.3).
  - There was limited evidence that longer participation is protective.
  - There was strong evidence that taking part in other sports does not change risk.
- Limits: only 3 cohorts, so the data could not be pooled. Most CrossFit injury studies are cross-sectional.

### 4.2 Rhabdomyolysis

**Hopkins et al. 2019**
- Citation: Hopkins BS, Li D, Svet M, Kesavabhotla K, Dahdaleh NS. CrossFit and rhabdomyolysis: a case series of 11 patients presenting at a single academic institution. *J Sci Med Sport.* 2019;22(7):758–762.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2019.01.019
- Design: retrospective record review at one hospital, 2010–2016.
- Sample: 523 patients with CrossFit-related injuries.
- Findings:
  - 11 patients (2.1%) had rhabdomyolysis.
  - 54.5% of those patients were beginners.
  - 90.9% had dark urine, and the average hospital stay was about 2.9 days.
- Limits: a single center and a case series. There is no count of all participants, so the true incidence is unknown. A published comment questions its conclusions (Dominski et al. 2019, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2019.04.011).

### 4.3 Training load monitoring and session RPE

**Foster et al. 2001**
- Citation: Foster C, Florhaug JA, Franklin J, et al. A new approach to monitoring exercise training. *J Strength Cond Res.* 2001;15(1):109–115.
- Link: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11708692/ (no DOI listed in PubMed)
- Design: two-part comparison of session RPE against a heart-rate-based load score.
- Sample: one group did steady and interval cycling, another did basketball practice. The group sizes are not in the abstract and the full text was not read.
- Finding: session RPE and the heart-rate method were "highly consistent" in both settings. The session-RPE score was higher in absolute terms. The authors call it "a valid method of quantitating exercise training during a wide variety of types of exercise."
- Method, as described by Haddad 2017: one rating of the whole session on Foster's modified CR-10 scale (0–10), multiplied by session minutes. The result is in "arbitrary units."
- [Unconfirmed] Timing. Later studies say Foster's rating is collected about 30 minutes after the session. The original full text was not checked.
- Limits: cycling and basketball only, not mixed-modal training.

**Haddad et al. 2017**
- Citation: Haddad M, Stylianides G, Djaoui L, Dellal A, Chamari K. Session-RPE method for training load monitoring: validity, ecological usefulness, and influencing factors. *Front Neurosci.* 2017;11:612.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2017.00612
- Design: review of studies from 2001 to 2016.
- Sample: 36 studies tested validity or reliability using the modified CR-10 scale.
- Finding: the method showed validity and "good reliability" across many sports, ages, and levels. Load = RPE × session duration in minutes.
- Limits: mostly team-sport and endurance studies, with no CrossFit studies in this period.

**Crawford et al. 2018**
- Citation: Crawford DA, Drake NB, Carper MJ, DeBlauw J, Heinrich KM. Validity, reliability, and application of the session-RPE method for quantifying training loads during high intensity functional training. *Sports (Basel).* 2018;6(3):84.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.3390/sports6030084
- Design: secondary analysis of a 9-week training study. Weeks 2–4 and 6–8 were used, so 6 weeks in total.
- Sample: 25 healthy men and women training 5 days per week. Sessions lasted about 1 hour (warm-up, daily workout, mobility) and were supervised.
- Scale and duration used:
  - The **original Borg 6–20 RPE scale**, not the CR-10.
  - The rating was collected **about 5 minutes after the daily workout**.
  - Load = RPE × **the daily workout's minutes only**, not the whole 1-hour class.
- Findings:
  - At the group level, session RPE correlated with Edwards' heart-rate load (r = 0.889) and with Banister's TRIMP (r = 0.561).
  - Individual correlations with Edwards' load ranged from 0.677 to 0.973.
  - Reliability was poor. The agreement between RPE and heart-rate quartile had an ICC of 0.586, and only 52% of ratings matched the heart-rate quartile.
  - Validity and reliability seemed to improve over time.
- Limits:
  - RPE was taken at one time point right after the workout, which may inflate it ("end spurt").
  - Only internal, heart-rate-based criteria were used. External work and heavy lifting days were not captured.
  - The sample was small.
  - Two authors hold CrossFit Level 1 certificates (declared).

**Falk Neto et al. 2020**
- Citation: Falk Neto JH, Tibana RA, de Sousa NMF, Prestes J, Voltarelli FA, Kennedy MD. Session rating of perceived exertion is a superior method to monitor internal training loads of functional fitness training sessions performed at different intensities when compared to training impulse. *Front Physiol.* 2020;11:919.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2020.00919
- Design: within-subject comparison of an all-out session against a session held at RPE 6 of 10.
- Sample: 8 trained men.
- Findings:
  - Session RPE separated the two sessions more clearly than heart-rate TRIMP methods.
  - Session RPE loads were 91.7 vs 42.6 AU (effect size 1.82).
  - Only session RPE correlated with blood lactate 30 minutes after the session.
- Limits: 8 men and two sessions. The authors say it is not yet shown to track fitness or fatigue over a training period.

**Impellizzeri et al. 2020**
- Citation: Impellizzeri FM, Tenan MS, Kempton T, Novak A, Coutts AJ. Acute:chronic workload ratio: conceptual issues and fundamental pitfalls. *Int J Sports Physiol Perform.* 2020;15(6):907–913.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2019-0864
- Design: conceptual and methodological analysis.
- Finding: "There is no evidence supporting the use of ACWR in training-load-management systems or for training recommendations aimed at reducing injury risk." The ACWR (acute:chronic workload ratio) compares this week's load with the average of recent weeks. The authors call it an inaccurate metric that "adds noise and creates statistical artifacts."
- Limits: this is an argument paper, not new data. It applies to any sport, CrossFit included.

### 4.4 Recovery between sessions

**Timón et al. 2019**
- Citation: Timón R, Olcina G, Camacho-Cardeñosa M, Camacho-Cardenosa A, Martinez-Guardado I, Marcos-Serrano M. 48-hour recovery of biochemical parameters and physical performance after two modalities of CrossFit workouts. *Biol Sport.* 2019;36(3):283–289.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.5114/biolsport.2019.85458
- Design: crossover. Each person did an AMRAP workout and a rounds-for-time workout on separate days.
- Sample: 12 trained CrossFit practitioners.
- Findings:
  - The rounds-for-time workout was more intense: lactate 18.4 vs 13.3 mmol/L, and mean heart rate 159.8 vs 127.6 beats per minute.
  - Both raised creatine kinase and liver enzymes and lowered plank-test performance.
  - All measures were back to baseline by 48 hours.
- Limits: 12 people, single workouts, and blood markers plus one simple performance test.

**Tibana et al. 2016**
- Citation: Tibana RA, de Almeida LM, Frade de Sousa NM, et al. Two consecutive days of CrossFit training affects pro and anti-inflammatory cytokines and osteoprotegerin without impairments in muscle power. *Front Physiol.* 2016;7:260. (Erratum 2018;9:771.)
- Link: https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2016.00260
- Design: two sessions 24 hours apart.
- Sample: 9 trained men with about 2.5 years of CrossFit experience.
- Finding: inflammatory markers changed, but back-squat power at 50% of 1RM was not impaired across the two days.
- Limits: 9 men, a light power test, and only two days.

**Kliszczewicz et al. 2018**
- Citation: Kliszczewicz B, Williamson C, Bechke E, McKenzie M, Hoffstetter W. Autonomic response to a short and long bout of high-intensity functional training. *J Sports Sci.* 2018;36(16):1872–1879.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2018.1423857
- Design: crossover comparing a workout of under 5 minutes with a 15-minute workout.
- Sample: 10 healthy men.
- Finding:
  - Heart rate variability (lnRMSSD, lnHF) dropped after both workouts and recovered by 2 hours.
  - Adrenaline and noradrenaline recovered by 1 hour.
  - Workout length made no difference.
- Limits: 10 men and acute measures only. It says nothing about multi-day fatigue.

**Morán-Navarro et al. 2017**
- Citation: Morán-Navarro R, Pérez CE, Mora-Rodríguez R, et al. Time course of recovery following resistance training leading or not to failure. *Eur J Appl Physiol.* 2017;117(12):2387–2399.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-017-3725-7
- Design: three bench press and squat protocols, measured from 12 hours before to 72 hours after.
- Sample: 10 resistance-trained men.
- Finding: sets to failure (3 × 10 of 10 possible) slowed recovery of speed and jump height for up to 24–48 hours. This held even against the same volume done without failure (6 × 5 of 10 possible).
- Limits: two lifts, 10 men, and not a CrossFit setting.

### 4.5 Concurrent training interference

Concurrent training means doing strength work and endurance work in the same program.

**Wilson et al. 2012**
- Citation: Wilson JM, Marin PJ, Rhea MR, Wilson SM, Loenneke JP, Anderson JC. Concurrent training: a meta-analysis examining interference of aerobic and resistance exercises. *J Strength Cond Res.* 2012;26(8):2293–2307.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0b013e31823a3e2d
- Design: meta-analysis.
- Sample: 21 studies, 422 effect sizes.
- Findings:
  - Strength effect sizes: strength only 1.76, concurrent 1.44, endurance only 0.78.
  - Power effect sizes: 0.91, 0.55, and 0.11 in the same order.
  - Running, but not cycling, reduced hypertrophy and strength gains.
  - Longer and more frequent endurance work related to smaller gains.
- Limits: older and varied studies. These were separate aerobic and lifting sessions, not mixed workouts.

**Schumann et al. 2022**
- Citation: Schumann M, Feuerbacher JF, Sünkeler M, et al. Compatibility of concurrent aerobic and strength training for skeletal muscle size and function: an updated systematic review and meta-analysis. *Sports Med.* 2022;52(3):601–612.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01587-7
- Design: systematic review and meta-analysis (PROSPERO CRD42020203777).
- Sample: 43 studies of supervised training lasting at least 4 weeks.
- Findings:
  - No meaningful loss in maximal strength (SMD −0.06) or muscle size (SMD −0.01).
  - Explosive strength was reduced (SMD −0.28). The loss was larger when both were done in the same session than when separated by at least 3 hours.
  - Type of aerobic work, frequency, training status, and age did not change the results.
- Limits: the studies tested aerobic training added to lifting, not CrossFit's mixed-modal workouts. Most programs were short.

**Murlasits et al. 2018**
- Citation: Murlasits Z, Kneffel Z, Thalib L. The physiological effects of concurrent strength and endurance training sequence: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *J Sports Sci.* 2018;36(11):1212–1219.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2017.1364405
- Design: meta-analysis of same-session order.
- Finding: strength first, then endurance, gave more lower-body 1RM gain: +3.96 kg (95% CI 0.81–7.10). Order did not change VO2max.
- Limits: small average effect, and the protocols were not CrossFit sessions.

### 4.6 Strength progression: novice versus trained lifters

**Rhea et al. 2003**
- Citation: Rhea MR, Alvar BA, Burkett LN, Ball SD. A meta-analysis to determine the dose response for strength development. *Med Sci Sports Exerc.* 2003;35(3):456–464.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1249/01.MSS.0000053727.63505.D4
- Design: meta-analysis.
- Sample: 140 studies, 1,433 effect sizes.
- Findings: responses differed by training status.
  - Untrained: the largest gains came at a mean of 60% of 1RM, 3 days per week per muscle group.
  - Trained: the largest gains came at 80% of 1RM, 2 days per week.
  - Both: 4 sets per muscle group.
- Limits: pooled averages from varied designs. These are not individual prescriptions. The paper predates CrossFit research.

**Peterson et al. 2004 and 2005**
- Citations: Peterson MD, Rhea MR, Alvar BA. Maximizing strength development in athletes: a meta-analysis to determine the dose-response relationship. *J Strength Cond Res.* 2004;18(2):377–382. Also: Applications of the dose-response for muscular strength development: a review of meta-analytic efficacy and reliability for designing training prescription. *J Strength Cond Res.* 2005;19(4):950–958.
- Links: https://doi.org/10.1519/R-12842.1 and https://doi.org/10.1519/R-16874.1
- Design: a meta-analysis of athletes (37 studies) and a synthesis of two meta-analyses (177 studies).
- Findings:
  - Athletes gained most at 85% of 1RM, 2 days per week, 8 sets per muscle group.
  - "Training status influences the requisite doses as well as the potential magnitude of response."
  - For gains beyond general health, the training dose must rise as experience rises.
- Limits: same method concerns as Rhea 2003.

**American College of Sports Medicine 2009**
- Citation: American College of Sports Medicine. Position stand: progression models in resistance training for healthy adults. *Med Sci Sports Exerc.* 2009;41(3):687–708.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181915670
- Design: consensus position stand.
- Recommendations:
  - Novices: 8–12 repetition maximum loads, 2–3 days per week.
  - Intermediate and advanced lifters: 1–12 repetition maximum in a periodized plan, with emphasis on heavy 1–6 repetition maximum loads and 3–5 minutes of rest. Intermediate lifters train 3–4 days per week and advanced lifters 4–5.
  - Add 2–10% load when the athlete can do 1–2 reps more than the target.
- Limits: a consensus document that is partly expert opinion, and now over 15 years old.

**Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2020**
- Citation: Androulakis-Korakakis P, Fisher JP, Steele J. The minimum effective training dose required to increase 1RM strength in resistance-trained men: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *Sports Med.* 2020;50(4):751–765.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-019-01236-0
- Design: systematic review and meta-analysis.
- Sample: 6 studies, with 5 in the meta-analysis.
- Finding: one set of 6–12 reps at about 70–85% of 1RM, 2–3 times per week, close to failure, for 8–12 weeks, raised squat and bench press 1RM in trained men. The overall gain was +12.09 kg. The authors call this "suboptimal, yet significant."
- Limits: men only, few studies, and unclear results for the deadlift, women, and highly trained athletes.

**Currier et al. 2023**
- Citation: Currier BS, Mcleod JC, Banfield L, et al. Resistance training prescription for muscle strength and hypertrophy in healthy adults: a systematic review and Bayesian network meta-analysis. *Br J Sports Med.* 2023;57(18):1211–1220.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2023-106807
- Design: Bayesian network meta-analysis of randomized trials.
- Sample: the strength network had 178 studies with 5,097 people.
- Finding: every program beat no training. Higher loads (above 80% of 1RM) gave the most strength. The top-ranked program used higher load, multiple sets, three times per week.
- Limits: the abstract does not separate novice from trained lifters. Most trials are short.

### 4.7 Gymnastics skill progression

Little direct evidence exists. No trials were found of CrossFit-style progressions such as banded pull-ups, negatives, or strict before kipping.

**Dinunzio et al. 2019**
- Citation: Dinunzio C, Porter N, Van Scoy J, Cordice D, McCulloch RS. Alterations in kinematics and muscle activation patterns with the addition of a kipping action during a pull-up activity. *Sports Biomech.* 2019;18(6):622–635.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1080/14763141.2018.1452971
- Design: acute biomechanics comparison of 5 strict pull-ups and 5 kipping pull-ups.
- Sample: 11 athletes.
- Finding: kipping raised core and hip-flexor activity and lowered biceps activity by about 27% of maximum. The authors suggest kipping may allow more reps through "reduced upper body fatigue."
- Limits: acute measures in a small sample. Not a training or injury study.

**Prinold and Bull 2016**
- Citation: Prinold JA, Bull AM. Scapula kinematics of pull-up techniques: avoiding impingement risk with training changes. *J Sci Med Sport.* 2016;19(8):629–635.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2015.08.002
- Design: observational motion analysis of three strict pull-up grips.
- Sample: 11 people who regularly do pull-ups.
- Finding: wide-grip and reverse-grip pull-ups showed movement patterns "linked with increased impingement risk." Weight-assisted front pull-ups "could be recommended for weaker participants" but need study.
- Limits: no injury outcomes, strict pull-ups only, and a small sample.

**Vigouroux and Devise 2024**
- Citation: Vigouroux L, Devise M. Pull-up performance is affected differently by the muscle contraction regimens practiced during training among climbers. *Bioengineering (Basel).* 2024;11(1):85.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.3390/bioengineering11010085
- Design: randomized groups (eccentric, isometric, plyometric, control) for 5 weeks, twice per week.
- Sample: 30 advanced to elite climbers.
- Finding: maximal pull-up strength rose 2.2–5.0% in all training groups, with no change in the controls. Eccentric and plyometric training improved speed and power.
- Limits: climbers, not CrossFit athletes. Small groups and a short program.

**Bruce et al. 2017**
- Citation: Bruce O, Moull K, Fischer S. Principal components analysis to characterise fatigue-related changes in technique: application to double under jump rope. *J Sports Sci.* 2017;35(13):1300–1309.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2016.1221523
- Design: motion capture of double-unders done to fatigue.
- Sample: 10 trained athletes.
- Finding: with fatigue, athletes used more shoulder internal rotation. The authors suggest this "may increase the risk of developing shoulder injuries."
- Limits: 10 people and no injury follow-up.

### 4.8 Benchmark norms and what predicts benchmark scores

**Mangine, Cebulla, and Feito 2018**
- Citation: Mangine GT, Cebulla B, Feito Y. Normative values for self-reported benchmark workout scores in CrossFit® practitioners. *Sports Med Open.* 2018;4(1):39.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-018-0156-x
- Design: cross-sectional. Public profiles on the CrossFit website were collected between May and August 2017.
- Sample: 133,857 profiles, of which 10,000 valid profiles were randomly chosen.
- Findings: mean ± SD by group. The first letter is the competitive age class the paper used: I = individual, M = masters, T = teens. The second letter is M for men or F for women.

| Workout | IM | IF | MM | MF | TM | TF |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fran (s) | 250 ± 106 | 331 ± 181 | 311 ± 138 | 368 ± 138 | 316 ± 136 | 334 ± 120 |
| Grace (s) | 180 ± 90 | 213 ± 96 | 213 ± 93 | 238 ± 100 | 228 ± 63 | 223 ± 69 |
| Helen (min) | 9.5 ± 1.9 | 11.1 ± 2.4 | 10.2 ± 2.0 | 11.5 ± 2.3 | 9.4 ± 1.6 | 12.7 ± 1.9 |
| Filthy 50 (min) | 24.4 ± 5.9 | 27.3 ± 6.9 | 26.7 ± 6.1 | 28.2 ± 6.0 | 25.9 ± 7.9 | 28.3 ± 8.1 |

  The paper also gives decile tables.
- Limits:
  - Scores were self-reported and not verified. The authors suggest checking them against observed performances.
  - Scores were treated as one standard per sex. Scaled entries could not be separated.
  - The data are from 2017.
  - Users who post scores may be fitter than average.

**Butcher et al. 2015**
- Citation: Butcher SJ, Neyedly TJ, Horvey KJ, Benko CR. Do physiological measures predict selected CrossFit® benchmark performance? *Open Access J Sports Med.* 2015;6:241–247.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.2147/OAJSM.S88265
- Design: cross-sectional lab and field testing.
- Sample: 14 Open or Regional athletes.
- Findings:
  - The CrossFit Total (1RM back squat + press + deadlift) was the only predictor that held up for Grace (r = −0.88, R² = 0.77) and Fran (r = −0.65, R² = 0.42).
  - Nothing predicted Cindy.
  - VO2max and Wingate power did not predict any of the three.
- Limits: 14 athletes and three workouts.

**Mangine et al. 2020**
- Citation: Mangine GT, Stratton MT, Almeda CG, et al. Physiological differences between advanced CrossFit athletes, recreational CrossFit participants, and physically-active adults. *PLoS One.* 2020;15(4):e0223548.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0223548
- Design: cross-sectional.
- Sample: 8 advanced competitors, 8 recreational CrossFit participants, and 7 active controls.
- Findings:
  - Advanced athletes had less body fat, more lean mass, more isometric strength, about 19% higher peak aerobic capacity, and better 3-minute cycling.
  - Recreational CrossFit participants did not differ from controls who did traditional lifting and cardio.
- Limits: very small groups and a cross-sectional design.

### 4.9 Competition preparation: tapering

**Bosquet et al. 2007**
- Citation: Bosquet L, Montpetit J, Arvisais D, Mujika I. Effects of tapering on performance: a meta-analysis. *Med Sci Sports Exerc.* 2007;39(8):1358–1365.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e31806010e0
- Design: meta-analysis of tapers in competitive athletes.
- Sample: 27 studies.
- Finding: the best strategy was a 2-week taper. Volume was cut exponentially by 41–60%, with intensity and frequency kept the same.
- Limits: published before any CrossFit research, so no CrossFit studies are included. The sport mix was not re-checked here. The Open repeats a workout each week for several weeks, which is not a single peak event.

### 4.10 Reviews of CrossFit as a whole

**Claudino et al. 2018**
- Citation: Claudino JG, Gabbett TJ, Bourgeois F, et al. CrossFit overview: systematic review and meta-analysis. *Sports Med Open.* 2018;4(1):11.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-018-0124-5
- Design: systematic review and meta-analysis.
- Sample: 31 articles reviewed, 4 meta-analyzed.
- Finding: "only two studies had a high level of evidence at low risk of bias." The meta-analysis found no significant effects. Early data link CrossFit with higher sense of community, satisfaction, and motivation.
- Limits: a thin evidence base at the time.

**Feito, Heinrich, Butcher, and Poston 2018**
- Citation: Feito Y, Heinrich KM, Butcher SJ, Poston WSC. High-intensity functional training (HIFT): definition and research implications for improved fitness. *Sports (Basel).* 2018;6(3):76.
- Link: https://doi.org/10.3390/sports6030076
- Design: narrative review and definition paper.
- Finding: proposes a working definition of HIFT as distinct from HIIT (high-intensity interval training). HIFT uses "constantly varied functional exercises and various activity durations that may or may not incorporate rest." It notes that less is known about HIFT's effects than about HIIT's.
- Limits: not a systematic review.

## 5. What the evidence does NOT establish

- [Research] **That CrossFit is safer or riskier than other training.** Nearly all injury data come from retrospective, self-reported surveys. Injury definitions differ. Training hours are often assumed. Reported incidence ranges from 0.2 to 18.9 per 1,000 hours (Rodríguez 2022; Feito 2018). No randomized trial compares injury rates.
- [Research] **Which risk factors cause injury.** Only 3 prospective cohorts exist (Mehrab 2023). Supervision, experience, competing, and body size are associations, not proven causes.
- [Research] **The true rate of rhabdomyolysis in CrossFit.** Case series have no count of all participants (Hopkins 2019).
- [Research] **That any training-load number predicts injury in CrossFit.** No study validates a load threshold or the acute:chronic ratio for HIFT. The ratio itself is not supported for injury prevention (Impellizzeri 2020).
- [Research] **That one athlete's session RPE matches their heart-rate effort.** Group-level validity is good, but individual agreement was only 52% in Crawford 2018. Session RPE on heavy-lifting days in HIFT is not validated.
- [Research] **Which RPE scale and timing to use in CrossFit.** Crawford 2018 used Borg 6–20 about 5 minutes after the workout, multiplied by workout minutes. Foster's method uses CR-10, rates the whole session, and is usually collected later. The two give numbers on different scales. Do not mix them in one athlete's history.
- [Official] / [Research] **That "preserve the stimulus" scaling gives the same adaptation as Rx.** This is CrossFit's guidance (L1 p. 77). No study tests it. The only related finding is that switching back and forth between Rx and scaled loads was linked with more injuries in one cohort (Mehrab 2023). That finding is limited evidence.
- [Official] / [Research] **That 3-on/1-off beats other schedules.** The claim comes from CrossFit's own observation (L1 pp. 71–72). CrossFit.com "never used this template" (L1 p. 71). No study compares the two schedules.
- [Research] **That benchmark norms apply to verified, standard-quality reps.** The only large norms are self-reported and unverified, from 2017 (Mangine 2018). Movement standards have also drifted, as with Elizabeth's clean style and Grace's intended power clean.
- [Research] **How much concurrent interference occurs in mixed-modal workouts.** The meta-analyses test separate aerobic and lifting programs. Explosive strength looks most at risk (Schumann 2022). Nothing measures this inside a CrossFit week.
- [Research] **Exact strength progression rates for CrossFit athletes.** Dose-response data come from lifting studies, not athletes who also do daily conditioning. The general pattern that trained lifters need more load and volume for smaller gains is supported (Rhea 2003; Peterson 2005; ACSM 2009). Specific weekly kilogram gains are not.
- [Research] **How to progress gymnastics skills.** There are no trials on banded pull-ups, negatives, kipping drills, muscle-up progressions, or handstand push-up progressions in CrossFit athletes. The rule to get a strict pull-up before kipping is official guidance (L1 p. 219), not a tested rule.
- [Research] **How many days of recovery a given workout needs.** CrossFit recovery studies use 9–12 trained men, single workouts, and blood markers (Timón 2019; Tibana 2016). They do not cover novices, women, masters athletes, or high-volume weeks.
- [Research] **How to taper for the Open.** Taper evidence comes from other sports before CrossFit research existed (Bosquet 2007).
- [Research] **What predicts Open ranking.** Studies are small (14 athletes in Butcher 2015; 8 per group in Mangine 2020) and cover a few benchmarks only.
